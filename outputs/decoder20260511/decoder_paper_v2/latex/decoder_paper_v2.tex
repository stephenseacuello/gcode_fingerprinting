\Title{Structured Multi-Head Decoding for Computer Numerical Control G-Code Recovery from Multi-Modal Sensor Embeddings: A Cross-Validated Per-Field Recoverability Study}

\newcommand{\orcidauthorA}{0009-0009-0292-9706}

\Author{Stephen S. Eacuello $^{1,}$*\orcidA{}, Romesh S. Prasad $^{1}$ and Manbir S. Sodhi $^{1}$}

\AuthorNames{Stephen S. Eacuello, Romesh S. Prasad and Manbir S. Sodhi}

\address{%
$^{1}$ \quad Department of Industrial Systems Engineering, University of Rhode Island, Kingston, RI 02881, USA}

\corres{Correspondence: seacuello@uri.edu}

\abstract{
Verifying that a CNC milling machine actually executed the program it was commanded to run is an open problem in operational-technology security and process verification: it requires reading the executing G-code instruction stream from sensor data alone, without trusting the controller. Sensor-based CNC monitoring has reliably classified machining-operation regimes and estimated tool wear, but per-instruction recovery has not been demonstrated. We address this gap with \modelname{}, a multi-head Transformer decoder operating on the frozen latent of a prior multi-modal sensor encoder. Each 64-second sensor window is mapped to its executing G-code line via per-row supervised targets; inference is grammar-constrained so that structurally invalid token transitions are pruned at runtime. Across five-fold file-level cross-validation on a 120-file corpus spanning nine machining-operation classes --- evaluated under an in-distribution closed-vocabulary regime in which $97.6\%$ of distinct test G-code lines, and $99.0\%$ of test line-instances, also appear in training of the same fold (Section~\ref{sec:lim-dataset}) --- the results factorise cleanly along a categorical-vs-numeric axis. \emph{Categorical} G-code structure --- the motion-command head (G0, G1, G2, G3, G53, M30), axis presence, and motion direction --- is recoverable from sensors alone at $0.8875 \pm 0.0558$, $0.9931 \pm 0.0036$, and $0.9888 \pm 0.0063$ accuracy with no positional metadata exposed to the decoder; these classification heads operate directly on the encoder memory and report identical accuracies under both teacher-forced and autoregressive decoding. \emph{Numeric} coordinate precision, by contrast, is constrained by exposure bias compounded along the autoregressive token stream: under teacher-forced sweep-time evaluation token accuracy reaches $0.7811 \pm 0.0216$ and numeric accuracy $0.5850 \pm 0.0331$, but under deployment-true autoregressive decoding the model converges to a corpus-modal token sequence and these metrics drop to $0.2148 \pm 0.0685$ and $0.1038 \pm 0.0322$ respectively. We delineate which components of a G-code stream a sensor-based decoder can and cannot reconstruct on present-day 4-Hz CNC sensor hardware, locate the remaining gap at the autoregressive sequence head rather than the encoder, and quantify the implications for tamper-detection: in the closed-vocabulary in-distribution regime, command-identity substitutions are recoverable at row-level TPR $0.92$--$0.95$ and FPR $0.22$--$0.30$, and a $K\!=\!10$-row majority-vote aggregator brings this to TPR $\sim\!0.96$ at an expected FPR of $0.05$--$0.15$ under realistic alert autocorrelation (best case $0.04$ under row-independence); under the open-vocabulary leave-one-class-out regime command accuracy itself falls to $0.21$, so these detection guarantees hold within the trained motion-pattern envelope, not for arbitrary novel toolpaths. Numeric-value and feed-rate substitutions within the within-window range are not reliably detectable on the present sensor stack.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A modern Computer Numerical Control (CNC) milling machine is a tightly choreographed system: motors drive a cutting tool along multi-axis toolpaths specified by a G-code program, with tolerances measured in micrometres. Every cut leaves a multi-modal sensor signature --- vibration through the machine frame, acoustic emission, motor-current draw, ambient temperature and illumination --- that is in principle informative about the instruction stream that produced it. Whether that signature is informative enough to \emph{recover} the instruction stream line by line, without access to the controller, is the question this paper takes up. The applications are direct: continuous process verification, post-hoc forensic audit of contested runs, and detection of malicious or accidental G-code substitution in increasingly networked manufacturing environments~\cite{nist2023sp80082,nist2024fmnet,sturm2014cyber,yampolskiy2018security,zhong2017intelligent,kimmell2025cnc}, where the cost of an undetected deviation in feed rate, depth of cut, or motion command can be a scrapped part, damaged tooling, or worse~\cite{teti2010advanced,serin2020tool}.

Multi-modal sensor monitoring of CNC machines is a mature subfield, but its targets have been broad. Tool-wear estimation~\cite{teti2010advanced,serin2020tool,zhu2009wavelet,mohanraj2020tool,wang2024multisensor} and operation-type classification~\cite{stathatos2024cnn,coelho2024anomaly,wang2018deep,zhao2019machine} answer session-level questions: \emph{is the tool worn?}, \emph{what kind of operation is running?} Their target labels are coarse --- a single value per recording, or a class per file. The finer question --- can the specific G-code instruction executing at this moment be identified from sensors alone? --- has been largely absent from the CNC literature, despite its direct implications for tamper detection, intellectual-property protection~\cite{alfaruque2016acoustic,song2016my,chhetri2016kcad,jamarani2024screg,chattopadhyay2025video}, and root-cause analysis on contested runs.

\paragraph{Why CNC milling is harder than the additive-manufacturing analogue.} The closest body of work is side-channel reconstruction of toolpaths in additive manufacturing~\cite{alfaruque2016acoustic,song2016my,chhetri2016kcad}, where Fused Deposition Modeling deposits material one axis at a time in a controlled environment, sensors can be placed freely without risk of damage, and the sensor-to-G-code mapping is comparatively low-noise. CNC milling is a substantially harder signal environment. Simultaneous multi-axis interpolation, nonlinear force-engagement relationships, structural vibration through the machine frame, and high-energy chip ejection corrupt sensor signals and physically constrain where sensors can be mounted~\cite{teti2010advanced,wang2018deep}. The mapping is also many-to-one: many G-code instructions produce broadly similar sensor signatures when their cutting parameters coincide, and the same instruction can be cut at different speeds, depths, or materials.

\paragraph{Building on prior work in our group.} The sensor encoder used in this study is a prior contribution that achieved 96.3\% accuracy on nine-class CNC operation classification using a multi-modal denoising autoencoder over six distributed sensor boards~\cite{encoder_paper}. That work established the encoder's representational competence at \emph{operation-class} granularity. A companion contribution defined a hybrid grammar-based tokenisation of G-code suitable for sequence models~\cite{grammar_paper}. The present paper takes the encoder as a fixed feature extractor and the grammar as a vocabulary, then asks what additional information the encoder's per-window latent carries about the row-level instructions that drove the cut.

\paragraph{Three challenges specific to this task.} Direct G-code generation from sensor signals raises issues that off-the-shelf sequence-to-sequence models~\cite{sutskever2014sequence,bahdanau2014neural,vaswani2017attention,graves2013speech} do not address.
(i)~A 64-second sensor window straddles tens to hundreds of G-code rows; a naive window-level target collapses this distribution into a single label and destroys row-level information.
(ii)~The numeric portion of the vocabulary contains thousands of bucket tokens, most absent from any one training fold, so a softmax over the full vocabulary is severely under-constrained.
(iii)~Naively-conditioned decoders readily exploit positional metadata --- window index, file identifier, total window count --- as shortcuts that inflate accuracy without proving sensor-driven recovery~\cite{meyes2019ablation,jia2016deep}. The architectural and training-target choices in Section~\ref{sec:method} address each of these directly.

\textbf{Research questions.} This study is guided by four research questions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Per-field recoverability.} Which components of a G-code line --- command identity, axis presence, motion direction, numeric value --- are recoverable from sensor data alone, and with what fidelity?
\item \textbf{The metadata-versus-sensor split.} Does exposing positional metadata (window index, file identifier) to the decoder improve recovery, and which fields benefit if so?
\item \textbf{Sensor-modality contribution.} Which of seven sensor groups (accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, environmental, color, audio-RMS, electrical) carry the strongest instruction-level signal?
\item \textbf{Cross-class generalization.} Does the decoder generalize to machining operation classes it never saw during training (leave-one-class-out)?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\arabic*)}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Per-row supervised target formulation.} Each sensor window is mapped to the single G-code line that fires within it, yielding approximately $\sim$19{,}500 training samples per fold versus $\sim$300 for a window-level alternative, while eliminating within-window label ambiguity.

\item \textbf{\modelname{} architecture.} A Transformer decoder with six structured prediction heads (token, type, motion-command, parameter-type, sign, digit) operating on the frozen latent of a prior multi-modal denoising encoder~\cite{encoder_paper}. Inference is grammar-constrained at the token head so that structurally invalid transitions are masked at runtime, following recent work on grammar-aware decoding~\cite{picard,outlines,xgrammar}.

\item \textbf{Per-axis recoverability characterization.} An evaluation that decomposes G-code into (X, Y, Z, F, S, R, I, J) axis fields and reports separately for each field whether (a) the axis is detected, (b) the motion direction is correct, and (c) the numeric value is recovered. This separation reveals which fields are sensor-recoverable and which are not.

\item \textbf{Ablation matrix.} Cross-validated ablations of (a) positional-metadata access, (b) per-row vs. full-window target formulation, (c) sequence-classifier prior, (d) vocabulary precision, and (e) each of seven sensor modalities. Methodology follows ablation-study practice in industrial neural-network research~\cite{meyes2019ablation,jia2016deep}.

\item \textbf{Cross-class generalization study.} A leave-one-class-out study across nine machining operation classes that stress-tests whether the decoder memorises per-class shortcuts or generalises to unseen toolpath strategies.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work in CNC monitoring, sequence-to-sequence modeling for manufacturing, grammar-constrained decoding, and side-channel recovery of manufacturing instructions. Section~\ref{sec:problem} formalizes the task, vocabulary, and evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the model architecture and training procedure. Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the dataset, preprocessing, and experimental protocol. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents the headline RQ1 result and a set of supporting analyses (per-axis recoverability, per-digit decomposition, per-operation-class breakdown, decoding-strategy sensitivity, output-position failure distribution, failure-mode analysis, and autoregressive mode collapse), then addresses RQ2--RQ4 in dedicated subsections. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the results and discusses limitations and deployment implications. Section~\ref{sec:future} outlines a designed-experiment data collection that targets the unrecovered G-code fields identified by this study.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We review four bodies of work that bear on the present study: sensor-based CNC monitoring and modality fusion (Section~\ref{sec:related-cnc}); sequence-to-sequence and grammar-constrained decoding (Section~\ref{sec:related-seq2seq}); side-channel reconstruction of manufacturing instructions (Section~\ref{sec:related-sidechan}); and manufacturing cybersecurity context (Section~\ref{sec:related-security}).

\subsection{CNC Process Monitoring and Multi-Modal Sensor Fusion}
\label{sec:related-cnc}

Sensor-based monitoring of machining processes is a mature subfield. Early work identified force, vibration, acoustic emission, temperature, and motor current as complementary modalities for tool-condition assessment~\cite{teti2010advanced}. Hand-crafted features extracted via Fourier and wavelet transforms became standard practice~\cite{zhu2009wavelet,mohanraj2020tool}. Deep learning enabled end-to-end feature learning from raw signals, reducing reliance on domain-specific feature engineering~\cite{wang2018deep,zhao2019machine}. Convolutional networks applied to vibration spectrograms and raw multi-channel signals achieved strong results on bearing fault detection~\cite{janssens2016convolutional}, surface-roughness classification~\cite{bhandari2021comparative,stathatos2024cnn}, and CNC anomaly detection~\cite{coelho2024anomaly}.

Multi-modal fusion strategies span early, late, and intermediate approaches~\cite{lahat2015multimodal,ramachandram2017deep}. Cross-modal attention has shown particular promise when modalities are complementary rather than redundant~\cite{cao2023crossattention,wang2024multisensor}. A comprehensive review by Tsanousa \etal~\cite{tsanousa2022multisensor} confirms that the field remains dominated by tool-condition and operation-classification tasks. Two gaps persist relative to instruction-level recovery. First, target labels in prior work are session-level (tool worn / not worn) or coarse class labels (turning vs.\ milling); per-row instruction targets are largely absent. Second, ablation studies that progressively remove sensor groups to quantify their marginal contribution~\cite{meyes2019ablation,jia2016deep} are themselves uncommon in CNC monitoring, leaving practitioners without quantitative guidance on which sensors carry instruction-level signal as distinct from operation-class signal.

\subsection{Sequence-to-Sequence Modeling and Grammar-Constrained Decoding}
\label{sec:related-seq2seq}

Sequence-to-sequence neural models were established for natural-language translation~\cite{sutskever2014sequence,bahdanau2014neural,vaswani2017attention} and have since been applied across speech~\cite{graves2013speech}, time series~\cite{wen2023transformers}, and robotics~\cite{robotics_s2s}. Long Short-Term Memory networks~\cite{hochreiter1997long} remain widely used for sequential industrial data where streaming inference and causal ordering matter~\cite{malhotra2015lstm}. Denoising autoencoders~\cite{vincent2010stacked} learn representations robust to input corruption --- a property particularly valuable when industrial sensors are subject to mechanical, electromagnetic, and thermal noise.

When the output sequence follows a known grammar, constrained decoding can prune structurally invalid tokens at inference time. Picard~\cite{picard}, Outlines~\cite{outlines}, and XGrammar~\cite{xgrammar} have demonstrated efficient runtime grammar masks for SQL and JSON generation. G-code is amenable to the same treatment: instructions consist of an optional command token followed by address-value pairs subject to first-order ordering constraints~\cite{grammar_paper,gcode_foundational}. Recent work on large-language-model code generation for additive manufacturing~\cite{gllm,image2gcode,chatcnc} addresses a related but distinct task --- generating G-code from natural-language descriptions or images --- and does not consider sensor-conditioned recovery.

\subsection{Side-Channel Reconstruction of Manufacturing Instructions}
\label{sec:related-sidechan}

The closest analog to this work is side-channel reconstruction of toolpaths in additive manufacturing. Al~Faruque \etal~\cite{alfaruque2016acoustic} recovered Fused Deposition Modeling print geometry from acoustic emissions, achieving high agreement with the source G-code. Song \etal~\cite{song2016my} showed that a smartphone microphone alone could recover sufficient information to reproduce printed objects. Chhetri \etal~\cite{chhetri2016kcad} extended the approach to a detection-oriented setting, identifying kinetic anomalies indicative of attacks. More recent work uses optical~\cite{chattopadhyay2025video} and combined acoustic-magnetic~\cite{jamarani2024screg} side-channels to reconstruct full part geometries from 3D printers. Sturm \etal~\cite{sturm2014cyber} and Yampolskiy \etal~\cite{yampolskiy2018security} survey cyber-physical attack taxonomies in additive manufacturing. We note one important methodological distinction: the AM side-channel literature typically uses \emph{raw acoustic emission} signals sampled at kHz--MHz rates, whereas the present work uses an \emph{audio-RMS envelope} scalar feature sampled at $4$~Hz. The audio-RMS feature retains the energy magnitude of the cutting sound but discards the spectral content that drives most prior AM-side-channel methods; the resulting per-instruction recovery is therefore not directly comparable to the AM literature and is more conservative in its sensor demands.

CNC milling poses a substantially harder sensor environment than additive manufacturing for the reasons stated in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}: simultaneous multi-axis motion, nonlinear force-engagement, and structural vibration. As a consequence, the additive-manufacturing recovery methods do not transfer directly. Kimmell \etal~\cite{kimmell2025cnc} demonstrated sensor-based detection of control-injection attacks in CNC machining using energy-anomaly thresholds, but their task is binary detection rather than instruction-level reconstruction. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work reports per-row G-code recovery from CNC sensor data with structured-output evaluation.

\subsection{Manufacturing Cybersecurity and Process Verification}
\label{sec:related-security}

The cybersecurity case for instruction-level sensor verification is direct. The machining-specific instruction-stream-integrity gap is articulated in the FMNet 2024 research-needs report~\cite{nist2024fmnet}; NIST SP 800-82 Rev.~3~\cite{nist2023sp80082} provides the general operational-technology framing, specifically its continuous-monitoring guidance under the Detect function and its supply-chain-risk guidance, against which an out-of-band physics-of-record monitor is a complementary control. Adversarial G-code substitution in PLC and CNC controllers is an established attack surface~\cite{zonouz2014plc,sturm2014cyber,yampolskiy2018security,kimmell2025cnc,goh2017dataset}. Reconstructing the actually-executed instruction stream from external sensors --- independent of the controller --- provides an integrity-checking baseline that cannot be subverted by controller-resident malware. The present work characterises which components of that instruction stream are realistically reconstructable on present-day sensor hardware, and which require additional sensor coverage or richer training data to recover.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Task Definition}
\label{sec:task}

Let $W \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times C}$ denote a sensor window of $T$ samples at $C$ channels, recorded over a fixed window length aligned to a CNC milling operation. Let $g$ denote the G-code line that was executing within $W$. The task is to learn a mapping
\begin{equation*}
  f_\theta: W \mapsto \hat{g}, \qquad \hat{g} = (\hat{t}_1, \hat{t}_2, \dots, \hat{t}_L),
\end{equation*}
where $\hat{g}$ is a sequence of $L$ G-code tokens drawn from a vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. The training objective minimises a multi-head cross-entropy loss with the auxiliary structured heads described in Section~\ref{sec:decoder}, plus an end-of-sequence token. At inference, the decoder generates tokens autoregressively under a grammar mask that zeros structurally invalid transitions in the legacy-token head.

\subsection{Vocabulary and Token Types}
\label{sec:problem-vocab}

We tokenise G-code with a hybrid scheme that disentangles structural primitives from numeric values~\cite{grammar_paper,gcode_foundational}. The vocabulary contains four token classes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SPECIAL tokens} --- \texttt{PAD}, \texttt{BOS}, \texttt{EOS}, \texttt{UNK}, \texttt{MASK}.
\item \textbf{COMMAND tokens} --- literal motion or machine commands (\texttt{G0}, \texttt{G1}, \texttt{G2}, \texttt{G3}, \texttt{M3}, \texttt{M5}, \texttt{M30}, \ldots).
\item \textbf{PARAM tokens} --- address letters that introduce an axis or rate parameter (\texttt{X}, \texttt{Y}, \texttt{Z}, \texttt{I}, \texttt{J}, \texttt{K}, \texttt{R}, \texttt{F}, \texttt{S}, \texttt{P}).
\item \textbf{NUMERIC tokens} --- bucketed numeric values, one bucket per (axis, magnitude) pair. The full vocabulary contains 1{,}048 \texttt{NUM\_X} buckets, 1{,}086 \texttt{NUM\_Y} buckets, and 231 \texttt{NUM\_Z} buckets, with smaller bucket sets for the remaining axes. Total vocabulary size: 2{,}418.
\end{itemize}

A typical G-code line in the corpus, e.g. \texttt{G1 X1.4919 Y1.4848 F22.}, becomes a token sequence \texttt{BOS G1 X NUM\_X\_1492 Y NUM\_Y\_1485 F NUM\_F\_22 EOS}. The bucketisation discretises numeric values to a fixed precision; the per-position digit head described in Section~\ref{sec:decoder} provides an auxiliary regression-style signal to mitigate the resulting discretisation loss.

\subsection{Per-Row Target Formulation}
\label{sec:per-row}

A naive supervised target assigns one G-code label per sensor window. In the present corpus, a 256-sample window at 4~Hz spans 64 seconds and contains an average of 60 distinct G-code rows (median 29, max 237). A window-level target therefore aggregates the information across these rows, destroying the row-level distinctions that distinguish, for instance, an \texttt{X} positive move from an \texttt{X} negative move within the same window. We instead emit one $(W, g_r)$ training pair per G-code row $r$ that fires within window $W$, where $g_r$ is the token sequence for that single row. This per-row formulation:

\begin{itemize}
\item Removes within-window label ambiguity. Each $(W, g_r)$ pair has exactly one correct target.
\item Increases the training-sample count by an order of magnitude ($\sim$19{,}500 per fold versus $\sim$300 for the window-level alternative).
\item Decouples the supervised target from the windowing schedule, preventing the model from inferring labels via positional shortcuts on \texttt{(window\_index, source\_file)}.
\end{itemize}

A complementary \emph{full-window} formulation, evaluated as an ablation (Section~\ref{sec:abl-target-mode}), retains one sample per window but uses a multi-line target consisting of all G-code rows that fired within the window, concatenated in temporal order. This formulation has fewer training samples per fold but a richer per-sample supervisory signal, and tests whether row-level autoregressive context (the previous row's tokens) closes any per-row recovery gaps.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

We report seven complementary metrics:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Token accuracy} --- proportion of non-padding token positions in the generated sequence whose argmax matches the target. The headline metric for sequence-quality.
\item \textbf{Sequence accuracy} --- proportion of test samples for which \emph{every} non-padding token matches the target. A strict exact-match metric.
\item \textbf{Type accuracy} --- per-token classification accuracy on the 4-way SPECIAL/COMMAND/PARAM/NUMERIC type head. Diagnoses whether the model is in the right structural mode at each step.
\item \textbf{Command accuracy} --- per-token classification accuracy on the 6-way motion-command head, evaluated only at positions where the target carries a COMMAND token.
\item \textbf{Parameter-type accuracy} --- per-token classification accuracy on the 10-way axis-letter head, evaluated at PARAM positions.
\item \textbf{Sign accuracy} --- per-position classification accuracy on the 3-way (positive, negative, absent) motion-sign head, evaluated at the relevant axis positions.
\item \textbf{Numeric (digit) accuracy} --- per-digit-position correctness pooled across all six digit positions in NUM tokens. The companion regression measure for numeric values is mean absolute error (MAE) of the reconstructed coordinate.
\end{enumerate}

Macro precision, recall, and F1 are computed for each classification head, using scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn} on the per-token confusion matrix.

\paragraph{Teacher-forced vs.\ autoregressive evaluation.} For any autoregressive sequence model, two evaluation regimes give materially different numbers and reflect different deployment scenarios.

\emph{Teacher-forced} (TF) evaluation supplies the ground-truth previous token at every position during the forward pass and measures the model's per-position classification accuracy under that ideal input. It is the standard objective during training and the standard sweep-time metric. \emph{Autoregressive} (AR) evaluation requires the model to consume its own previous predictions at each step, with no access to ground truth, and measures end-to-end generation quality under the actual deployment condition where no controller log is available to anchor the sequence.

The categorical heads in our decoder (type, command, parameter-type, sign) are independent classifiers on the encoder memory and therefore report identical accuracies under TF and AR. The autoregressive-token heads (legacy-token, digit-value, and the derived sequence and numeric metrics) report substantially lower accuracies under AR than under TF, as exposure-bias errors compound along the generated sequence.

Throughout this paper we explicitly distinguish the two regimes. The headline result table reports both regimes for the autoregressive heads, since the TF numbers correspond to the standard sweep-time evaluation used during hyperparameter selection while the AR numbers correspond to the realistic deployment scenario in which the decoder must recover G-code without a controller-log reference. For the categorical heads, a single number suffices because the regime distinction does not apply.

The system has three stages (Figure~\ref{fig:system-overview}). (i)~A multi-modal sensor encoder maps a (256-sample, 98-channel) sensor window into a (256-step, 256-dim) memory sequence. (ii)~A Transformer decoder consumes this memory via cross-attention and generates a token sequence representing the G-code line that was executing within the window. (iii)~A grammar mask zeros structurally invalid transitions in the decoder's softmax distribution at inference time. The encoder is frozen for all experiments in this paper; its training is described in a separate contribution~\cite{encoder_paper} and summarised here only insofar as it influences the decoder.

\subsection{Sensor Encoder (Frozen)}
\label{sec:encoder}

The encoder is a multi-modal denoising autoencoder coupled to a temporal LSTM (\encname{}). It accepts seven modality groups --- accelerometer (3-axis, 6 boards = 18 channels), gyroscope (18), magnetometer (18), environmental temperature (6), ambient color (24), audio RMS (6), and motor current (8) --- for a total of $C=98$ channels. A per-modality projection layer maps each modality into a shared $d=256$ space; learned modality-weighting gates~\cite{lahat2015multimodal,ramachandram2017deep} produce a fused representation; a multi-head self-attention stack~\cite{vaswani2017attention} captures cross-channel interactions; a denoising reconstruction objective~\cite{vincent2010stacked} encourages noise-robust representations; and a final two-layer LSTM~\cite{hochreiter1997long,malhotra2015lstm} produces the temporal memory consumed by the decoder. The full encoder has 8.6~M parameters and was trained for 30 epochs on the same file-level 5-fold splits used here, reaching 96.3\% test-set accuracy on the same nine-class operation-type task~\cite{encoder_paper}. The encoder is frozen for all decoder experiments and serves as a fixed feature extractor. Figure~\ref{fig:sensor_gcode_overlay} shows the raw input the encoder receives for one test window, with the seven modality groups aligned in time against the G-code instructions that drove the cut.

\subsection{Decoder Architecture}
\label{sec:decoder}

\modelname{} is an 8-layer Transformer decoder with $d_{\text{model}}=384$ and $h=12$ attention heads per layer. Each decoder layer consists of (a)~masked multi-head self-attention over the partial output sequence, (b)~multi-head cross-attention over the encoder memory, (c)~a position-wise feed-forward network with GELU activation, and (d)~residual connections with pre-layer normalisation~\cite{ba2016layernorm}. Dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} of $0.1$ is applied after each sub-layer. The decoder has $31.28$~M trainable parameters at the headline configuration (the Transformer stack is $18.9$~M; the remainder is the grammar-mask + sensor-prior machinery and the six output heads, embedding, and positional encodings --- component breakdown in Appendix~\ref{app:repl-inference}); with the frozen $8.6$~M encoder the total system size is $\approx\!39.9$~M parameters.

\subsubsection{Multi-Head Structured Output}
\label{sec:multihead}

Six prediction heads share the decoder's final hidden state at each output position. Each head is a linear projection followed by a softmax (or a six-position softmax in the case of the digit head). The heads are trained jointly with a weighted cross-entropy loss:
\begin{equation*}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{decoder}} =
    \lambda_{\text{tok}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{tok}} +
    \lambda_{\text{type}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{type}} +
    \lambda_{\text{cmd}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cmd}} +
    \lambda_{\text{param}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{param}} +
    \lambda_{\text{digit}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{digit}} +
    \lambda_{\text{sign}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{sign}}.
\end{equation*}
Default weights are $\lambda_{\text{tok}}=3.0$ and all auxiliary $\lambda=1.0$. The auxiliary heads serve two purposes: they regularise the shared representation toward token-type-aware features, and they expose structural prediction errors that would otherwise be hidden inside aggregate token accuracy.

\paragraph{Legacy token head.} A vocabulary-sized softmax (2{,}418 classes) operating on the decoder's final hidden state. This head is the primary inference output: at each timestep, the next token is drawn from this distribution. A grammar mask (Section~\ref{sec:grammar}) zeros structurally invalid transitions before the softmax.

\paragraph{Type head.} A 4-way classifier over \{SPECIAL, COMMAND, PARAM, NUMERIC\} on each output position. Trained against the type of the target token at that position. This is the coarsest classification head and the easiest task; its accuracy is therefore an upper bound on the structural sanity of the decoded sequence.

\paragraph{Motion-command head.} A 6-way classifier over \{G0, G1, G2, G3, G53, M30\}, evaluated against the target's command token at COMMAND positions. Distinguishes rapid moves (G0), linear cuts (G1), clockwise/counter-clockwise arcs (G2/G3), the work-coordinate-cancel command (G53), and program-end (M30). G53 and M30 have small per-fold supports ($n \le 90$); the per-class F1 in Table~\ref{tab:per_class_command} is correspondingly underpowered for these classes (see Section~\ref{sec:lim-power}).

\paragraph{Parameter-type head.} A 10-way classifier over \{X, Y, Z, I, J, K, R, F, S, P\}, evaluated against the target's address letter at PARAM positions. On the present corpus only X, Y, Z, R, and F have positive support in the test splits (see Table~\ref{tab:data_coverage} for per-field frequencies); the remaining trained labels (I, J, K, S, P) do not appear in the test data and therefore do not have per-class entries in Table~\ref{tab:per_class_param_type}.

\paragraph{Sign head.} A 3-way classifier over \{POSITIVE, NEGATIVE, ABSENT\} that predicts the sign of the most recent axis-value. This head provides direct supervision on motion direction independently of the discretised numeric value.

\paragraph{Per-position digit head.} A six-position 11-way classifier (digits 0--9 plus PAD) that reconstructs the numeric value of each NUM token digit-by-digit. The six positions cover the precision range observed in the corpus (typically $0\!-\!2\!.X\!X\!X\!X$ for an X or Y coordinate). Decomposing numeric reconstruction by digit position makes the manuscript's per-position recoverability analysis (Section~\ref{sec:numeric-decomposition}) possible and provides a regression-style auxiliary signal that complements the discretised NUM-token head.

\subsubsection{Grammar-Constrained Decoding}
\label{sec:grammar}

G-code follows a regular grammar: a line starts with an optional command (G/M) token; each address letter is followed by exactly one numeric value; certain commands (e.g.\ G2, G3) require specific subsequent fields. We encode the local part of this grammar as a static $|V| \times |V|$ binary transition mask and apply it to the legacy-token logits at each inference step, zeroing out structurally invalid transitions: BOS must be followed by a command or PARAM letter; a PARAM letter must be followed by its matching NUM token; a NUM token must be followed by another PARAM letter, a command, or EOS. The mask reduces, on average, the valid next-token set from $|V|=$ 2{,}418 to 31.8 tokens, a reduction that improves both calibration and exact-match accuracy. Implementation follows the runtime grammar-mask pattern established in Picard~\cite{picard}, Outlines~\cite{outlines}, and XGrammar~\cite{xgrammar}.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations of the local mask.} The bigram mask above does not encode command-state-dependent rules of the form ``the active command is G0, therefore the next address letter must not be R'' (R is an arc-radius parameter valid only with G2/G3). Such rules require a finite-state grammar (FSM) over a multi-token history. A separate inference-time FSM layer encodes the following higher-order rules on top of the bigram mask: G0 and G1 (linear moves) forbid R, I, and J; M30 (program end) forbids any subsequent command or PARAM letter. The FSM tracks the most recently emitted move-command as state and zeros forbidden transitions in the legacy-token logits before the softmax. The FSM is applied at autoregressive inference only; teacher-forced training is unaffected because the supervisory signal already encodes correct grammatical structure through the targets.

\paragraph{FSM ablation.} We audited grammar compliance and accuracy with and without the FSM layer, on both the baseline and the with-shortcuts 5-fold checkpoints under greedy autoregressive decoding. The bigram-only mask permits G0/G1 to emit R/I/J: across approximately 167{,}400 generated tokens per configuration, it produces 329 V1 violations affecting $170 / 549$ baseline samples ($31\%$) and 333 violations affecting $139 / 549$ with-shortcuts samples ($25\%$). With the FSM layer active, V1 violations drop to $0$ on baseline ($100\%$ reduction) and $11$ on with-shortcuts ($97\%$ reduction; the residual $11$ are G53/G55/G17/G90/G94 followed by R, a class the present FSM does not yet encode and a candidate for a follow-up grammar refinement). The 5-fold accuracy cost is $-0.27$~pp / $-0.41$~pp on baseline token / numeric, and $-0.04$~pp / $+0.01$~pp on with-shortcuts --- in both configurations the difference is within fold-to-fold noise (baseline std $\pm 0.069$ / $\pm 0.032$; with-shortcuts std $\pm 0.016$ / $\pm 0.017$). The FSM therefore eliminates the dominant class of grammar violation at no measurable accuracy cost. A paired-sample comparison across the 5 folds confirms statistical non-significance: paired-$t$ tests on baseline token / numeric give $p=0.72 / 0.51$ and Wilcoxon signed-rank gives $p=1.00 / 0.44$; on with-shortcuts token / numeric the same tests give $p=0.83 / 0.95$ and $p=0.63 / 1.00$ (\texttt{audit/fsm\_vs\_bigram\_paired\_stats.json}). M30-followup violations (V2) were $0$ in all configurations because M30 is rare in the corpus and the model learns termination from training. We adopt the FSM as the default deployment-time decoding configuration on this basis.

\subsubsection{Scheduled Sampling}
\label{sec:scheduled-sampling}

The decoder is trained with teacher forcing as default. Optionally a scheduled-sampling regime~\cite{scheduled_sampling} is applied: with probability $p$ that linearly increases from $0$ to a tuned maximum $p_{\text{max}}$ over training, each input position is replaced with the model's own previous prediction rather than the ground-truth target. This reduces exposure bias between training and inference. The maximum $p_{\text{max}}$ is treated as a hyperparameter and tuned by sweep (Section~\ref{sec:hp-sweep}).

\subsection{Training Configuration}
\label{sec:training}

The decoder is optimised with AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} ($\eta=$ $5 \times 10^{-5}$, weight decay 0.05) for up to 300 epochs with early stopping on validation token accuracy (patience 75). The first 10 epochs use a linear warm-up. Batch size is 64, mini-batches are shuffled per epoch, and gradient clipping is applied at norm 1.0. Best-checkpoint-by-validation-token-accuracy is retained.

The decoder is trained \emph{without} positional metadata in its baseline configuration: \texttt{window\_index}, \texttt{total\_windows}, and \texttt{source\_file} identifiers are not exposed to the decoder. Section~\ref{sec:abl-positional} ablates this choice. Figure~\ref{fig:learning_curves} shows the 5-fold training and validation loss / token-accuracy trajectories.

\subsection{Encoder Information-Content Probe}
\label{sec:encoder-probe}

Because the encoder is frozen, the decoder is bounded above by what the
encoder's representation actually carries. To characterise this upper bound
independently of the decoder, we train simple MLP probes directly on the
mean-pooled encoder memory and ask: \emph{given only the encoder's
output, what G-code field information is linearly (or shallowly)
recoverable from a window-level summary?} Figure~\ref{fig:encoder_tsne} visualises the qualitative answer in two dimensions: a t-SNE projection of the mean-pooled encoder memory shows that the 9 operation classes form tight, well-separated clusters in encoder-memory space.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{encoder_memory_tsne.pdf}
\caption{t-SNE projection of the mean-pooled frozen-encoder memory across all 5 test folds (549 windows), coloured by operation class. The 9 classes form tight, well-separated clusters, confirming that the encoder representation cleanly discriminates the operation-class level of the task. Within-class sub-structure (multiple sub-clusters per class) indicates the encoder preserves finer-than-class variation, but the downstream autoregressive decoder is unable to convert this within-class variation into sample-specific predictions, producing the class-conditional mode-collapse pattern characterised in Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}.}
\label{fig:encoder_tsne}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Linear structure and effective dimensionality.} The t-SNE projection is a nonlinear embedding; to characterise the representation's linear structure we additionally run PCA on the same mean-pooled memory matrix ($549 \times 256$; \texttt{audit/encoder\_memory\_pca.json}; Figure~\ref{fig:encoder_memory_pca}). The encoder memory is strongly low-rank: of the $256$ ambient dimensions, only $9$ principal components capture $80\%$ of the variance, $24$ capture $95\%$, and $40$ capture $99\%$. The first two PCs already separate the operation classes linearly (consistent with the categorical-head success: the class-discriminative signal lives in a low-dimensional, linearly-accessible subspace). The corollary is that fine-grained per-row numeric detail --- which would have to live in the long low-energy tail of the spectrum --- is poorly preserved relative to the dominant class-level structure, which is the representational counterpart of the numeric-recovery bottleneck reported in Section~\ref{sec:numeric-decomposition}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{encoder_memory_pca.pdf}
\caption{PCA of the mean-pooled frozen-encoder memory (549 test windows, all 5 folds). \emph{Left:} linear PC1--PC2 projection coloured by operation class --- the classes are linearly separable, the linear analogue of the t-SNE in Figure~\ref{fig:encoder_tsne}. \emph{Right:} cumulative explained-variance curve. The representation is low-rank: $9$ PCs reach $80\%$, $24$ PCs reach $95\%$, $40$ PCs reach $99\%$ of the variance in a $256$-dimensional ambient space. The dominant low-rank structure carries the class-level signal the categorical heads exploit; the fine numeric detail lives in the low-energy tail and is the representational bottleneck for exact coordinate recovery.}
\label{fig:encoder_memory_pca}
\end{figure}

Specifically, for each of (command identity, axis-presence, motion sign,
operation type), we mean-pool the encoder's $(T, d_{\text{model}})=
(256, 256)$ memory along the time axis to produce a single $256$-dimensional
vector per window. A two-hidden-layer MLP (hidden dim 256, ReLU, dropout 0.2)
is trained on the train split, model selection is on val, and accuracy is
reported on test. For continuous targets (numeric coordinate values),
the same probe is trained with an MSE objective and reported as RMSE.

\paragraph{What the probe tells us.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Window-level signal is available.} The probe recovers
operation type at $0.846$, axis presence at
0.87--0.99 across X/Y/Z, and motion sign at 0.80--0.86. The encoder
preserves enough information about each window to determine what is
happening at the operation-class level and what axes are active.

\item \textbf{Per-row disambiguation is not available.} A 64-second
sensor window in this corpus contains a median of 29 G-code rows (max
237). The mean-pooled encoder output is therefore the \emph{same} vector
for every row within the window, while the row-level supervised target
varies. The probe's accuracy is upper-bounded by the modal-row-per-window
ceiling --- approximately 0.85 for command, on this corpus. Probe
accuracies below the ceiling reflect within-window heterogeneity that
the encoder summary cannot resolve.

\item \textbf{Numeric value precision is lost.} The probe's RMSE on
X-value recovery (0.97) is close to the within-window value range
($\sim$3.0 normalised units), indicating that the encoder retains a
window-level mean of X but not the per-row precision that distinguishes
adjacent rows. RMSE on Y is 0.80 (slightly better); RMSE on Z is 0.10
(within-window Z range is small).
\end{itemize}

Two consequences inform the rest of the manuscript. First, the per-row
decoder cannot do better than the modal-row ceiling on categorical heads
without additional row-identification signal --- exactly the formulation
gap motivating the full-window comparison in Section~\ref{sec:abl-target-mode}.
Second, encoder retraining with row-level supervision is the natural
remedy for the numeric-precision bottleneck (Section~\ref{sec:future}).
Full probe results are in \texttt{audit/encoder\_probe\_v8.json}.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sweep}
\label{sec:hp-sweep}

The training configuration was selected through a three-stage hyperparameter sweep on fold 1.
\textbf{Stage 1} (8 cells) explored learning rate, batch size, model dimension, and loss-weighting on a coarse grid.
\textbf{Stage 2} (12 cells) refined around the Stage-1 winner by varying training techniques: curriculum learning, scheduled sampling, label smoothing~\cite{szegedy2016inception}, dropout, layer depth, and focal loss~\cite{focal_loss}.
\textbf{Stage 3} (14 cells) tested architectural variants: encoder fine-tuning, pointer-network output, regression-head numeric output, cross-window attention, multi-seed variance, and alternative encoder configurations.
The composite winner across all 34 cells (selected on a weighted-geometric-mean score) was used as the configuration for the 5-fold experiments reported in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

The corpus consists of 120 aligned CSV files recorded from a Bantam Tools desktop CNC milling machine~\cite{encoder_paper}. Six distributed multi-sensor boards (\texttt{frame\_l2}, \texttt{frame\_l3}, \texttt{frame\_r2}, \texttt{spindle2}, \texttt{y\_bed\_3}, \texttt{y\_bed\_4}) sample synchronously at 4~Hz. Each file carries:
\begin{itemize}
\item Accelerometer (3 axes $\times$ 6 boards = 18 channels);
\item Gyroscope (18 channels);
\item Magnetometer (18 channels);
\item Ambient color sensing (RGBA $\times$ 6 boards = 24 channels);
\item Environmental temperature (6 channels);
\item Audio root-mean-square (6 channels);
\item Motor-current draw (8 channels);
\end{itemize}
plus an aligned per-sample G-code line. We exclude proximity and pressure channels following the consistency audit reported in the encoder paper~\cite{encoder_paper}, retaining $C=98$ sensor channels.

The dataset spans nine machining operation classes: \texttt{adaptive}, \texttt{adaptive150025}, \texttt{face}, \texttt{face150025}, \texttt{pocket}, \texttt{pocket150025}, \texttt{damageadaptive}, \texttt{damageface}, \texttt{damagepocket}. The \texttt{150025} suffix denotes a variant feed/spindle parameter; \texttt{damage} prefixes denote operations executed on a tool that subsequently produced surface defects. These nine classes cover three motion strategies (face, pocket, adaptive) crossed with parameter and condition variants.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Windowing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

Sliding sensor windows of $T=256$ samples (64 seconds at 4~Hz) at stride 64 yield approximately $\sim$1{,}500 windows per fold. Each window is centred on a region of continuous cutting and aligned to its overlapping G-code rows. The per-row target formulation (Section~\ref{sec:per-row}) emits one $(W, g_r)$ pair per G-code row $r$ that fires within window $W$, yielding $\sim$19{,}500 training pairs, $\sim$7{,}000 validation pairs, and $\sim$7{,}500 test pairs per fold.

\subsection{Five-Fold File-Level Stratified Cross-Validation}
\label{sec:cv}

We use file-level stratified five-fold cross-validation to prevent within-file leakage. Each operation class is divided across folds in proportion to its frequency, so each fold contains representative coverage of all nine classes. Within a fold, train and validation splits come from the same fold partition; the test split is the held-out partition. We then run a G-code-line \emph{coverage repair} step (Appendix~\ref{app:coverage-repair}): any G-code line that appears in the test split but not the training partner triggers a file swap between the partners, until every test-line also appears in training. This step prevents the decoder from being evaluated on tokens it has never seen at training time, which would otherwise inflate the apparent generalisation gap. We note that the preprocessing uses overlapping sensor windows (window $256$, stride $64$, i.e., $75\%$ overlap between adjacent windows from the same file); within a single file, adjacent windows are therefore not statistically independent. File-level disjointness across folds is preserved, but the effective number of independent observations within a fold is smaller than the raw window count. The covariate-shift audit (Section~\ref{sec:lim-covariate}) and bootstrap confidence intervals on the 5-fold means use the per-fold mean as the unit of analysis, which sidesteps the within-fold non-independence.

\subsection{Baselines and Ablation Designs}
\label{sec:ablations-design}

We compare \modelname{} against the following ablations and baselines, each trained under identical preprocessing, splits, and hyperparameter configuration:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No-shortcuts baseline} (Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}). The headline configuration with positional metadata not exposed to the decoder.
\item \textbf{With-shortcuts ablation} (Section~\ref{sec:abl-positional}). The same model with \texttt{window\_index}, \texttt{total\_windows}, and \texttt{source\_file} hash exposed as additional decoder inputs. Tests whether positional metadata inflates accuracy beyond what sensor signal alone can recover.
\item \textbf{Full-window target ablation} (Section~\ref{sec:abl-target-mode}). The same encoder but with multi-line per-window targets instead of per-row targets. Tests whether autoregressive row-to-row context within a window resolves the per-row ambiguity discussed in Section~\ref{sec:per-row}.
\item \textbf{Sensor-modality ablation} (Section~\ref{sec:abl-sensors}). For each of the seven sensor groups, we zero the corresponding channels at the encoder input and evaluate the resulting decoder. Pooled across five folds for the gyroscope and color modalities; fold-1 only for the others.
\item \textbf{Sequence-classifier (pattern-aware) ablation} (Section~\ref{sec:abl-pattern}). A sequence-classifier head trained jointly with the legacy-token head predicts which whole G-code line the model is reconstructing and biases the token distribution toward that line's tokens.
\item \textbf{Vocabulary-precision ablation} (Section~\ref{sec:abl-vocab}). A coarser 2-digit numeric bucketing reduces vocabulary size by approximately $5\times$.
\item \textbf{Noise-augmentation ablation} (Section~\ref{sec:noise-aug}). Gaussian noise injection on sensor channels, feature dropout, and token masking during training~\cite{srivastava2014dropout,jia2016deep,meyes2019ablation}.
\item \textbf{Leave-one-class-out generalisation} (Section~\ref{sec:loco}). For each of the nine operation classes, train without that class in the training partition and evaluate on its held-out test samples.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:stats}

For each five-fold ablation, we report mean and standard deviation across folds, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) against the no-shortcuts baseline, and Cohen's $d$ effect size. ANOVA is computed with SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}; bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals use 10,000 resamples. Significance levels are reported as $\ast\,p<0.05$, $\ast\ast\,p<0.01$, $\ast\ast\ast\,p<0.001$.

\subsection{Software and Compute}
\label{sec:software}

The decoder is implemented in PyTorch and trained on NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. Preprocessing and analysis use scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}, XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} (metadata-baseline confounds), and SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}. Hyperparameter sweeps were tracked with Weights and Biases; full code and configuration files are released with the manuscript.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

We address the four research questions stated in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. First, axis-presence detection is uniformly high: X, Y, Z have perfect 5-fold has-axis accuracy ($1.000 \pm 0.000$), R reaches $0.994 \pm 0.012$, and F reaches $0.960 \pm 0.023$ on its thin support (Table~\ref{tab:per_axis}). \subsection{Per-Operation-Class Recoverability}
\label{sec:per-op-class}

Beyond pooled accuracy, the autoregressive predictions stratify dramatically by the operation class of the test window. Figure~\ref{fig:per_op_class} breaks down token and numeric AR accuracy across the 9 cutting-operation classes in the corpus. A single class --- \texttt{face} ($n=144$ test windows) --- reaches token accuracy $0.56$ and numeric accuracy $0.40$, substantially above the pooled headline of $0.215 / 0.104$. Every other operation class is stuck in the $0.09$--$0.25$ token / $0.01$--$0.15$ numeric range. This is the per-class signature of the mode-collapse mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}: the corpus-modal token sequence the decoder converges to under AR \emph{is} a facing-pattern toolpath, so \texttt{face} windows score well by coincidental agreement while other classes score poorly because the modal output is unrelated to their actual toolpath. The class with the most test samples per fold is \texttt{pocket150025} ($n=124$) and it sits at $0.09 / 0.01$ --- categorically incompatible with the modal facing-pattern. This reinforces the deployment recommendation in Section~\ref{sec:disc-when-to-use}: per-row mode avoids the long-sequence-length amplification of the modal-collapse effect.

\label{sec:decoding-strategy}

\subsection{Test-Time Sensor-Noise Sensitivity}
\label{sec:noise-sensitivity}

To characterise how robust the categorical heads are to deployment-time sensor degradation, we re-evaluate fold 1 of the baseline 5-fold checkpoint while injecting additive Gaussian noise into the frozen-encoder memory at varying $\sigma$ relative to the memory's empirical standard deviation (\texttt{audit/test\_time\_noise\_sensitivity.json}). This is a noise-robustness experiment on the \emph{encoder-to-decoder interface}, not on the \emph{raw sensor inputs}: noise injected at the memory level probes how stable the decoder is to perturbations in its conditioning vector but does not characterise robustness to raw-sensor degradation (the encoder is non-linear and may not propagate input noise to memory noise in a simple way). A raw-sensor-input noise sweep is part of the planned follow-up. The categorical heads tested at the memory level are exceptionally robust: across $\sigma \in [0, 2]$ (i.e., up to twice the magnitude of the signal itself), command-head accuracy varies from $0.78$ to $0.82$, type accuracy stays at $0.97$, and parameter-type accuracy stays at $0.98$ (Figure~\ref{fig:test_time_noise}). The slight upward drift on the command head is consistent with random noise acting as a label-smoothing regulariser, pushing predictions toward the modal class (G1) on borderline cases. The sign head is not included in Figure~\ref{fig:test_time_noise} because the sign-target alignment used in the audit JSON does not match the alignment used for the headline sign-accuracy report in Table~\ref{tab:headline}, and at $\sigma=0$ the audit gives an artefactually flat $0.10$ rather than the $0.989$ headline (\texttt{audit/test\_time\_noise\_sensitivity.json}); we therefore omit it pending a re-run with the corrected sign-target indexing. The other three heads validate cleanly against their headline fold-1 numbers at $\sigma=0$. The qualitative conclusion is that the categorical-recovery story this paper reports does not degrade gracefully but holds with surprising robustness to encoder-memory degradation; whether this robustness extends to raw-sensor noise on the input side is a follow-up question.

\subsection{Calibration of the Categorical Heads}
\label{sec:calibration}

A high-accuracy classifier may still be poorly calibrated --- its predicted-class confidence may systematically over- or under-estimate the empirical accuracy at that confidence. For a deployment that surfaces row-level instruction predictions to a human operator or to a downstream verification rule, calibrated confidence matters as much as accuracy: a $0.99$-confidence prediction should be correct $99\%$ of the time. We first report the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Maximum Calibration Error (MCE) at $10$ bins on the fold-1 baseline test set ($n_{\text{cmd}} = 1{,}982$ for command positions, $n_{\text{type}} = 37{,}590$ for all type positions, $n_{\text{pt}} = 35{,}392$ for parameter-type positions); these fold-1 numbers and the reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:reliability_diagram}) are \emph{illustrative} of the worst (outlier) fold, and the deployment-relevant figures are the cross-fold means reported in the next paragraph. Results: type head ECE $= 0.015$, MCE $= 0.037$ at $0.968$ accuracy and $0.954$ mean confidence; parameter-type ECE $= 0.020$, MCE $= 0.114$ at $0.985$ accuracy and $0.964$ mean confidence; command head ECE $= 0.122$, MCE $= 0.207$ at $0.780$ accuracy and $0.902$ mean confidence (\texttt{audit/calibration\_fold1.json}; Figure~\ref{fig:reliability_diagram}). The type and parameter-type heads are well-calibrated by the conventional thresholds ($\text{ECE} < 0.025$). The command head is moderately mis-calibrated and \emph{overconfident}: its mean predicted confidence exceeds its empirical accuracy by $12$~pp, with the largest gap of $20.7$~pp concentrated in the highest-confidence bin. The overconfidence is consistent with the LOCO collapse pattern in Section~\ref{sec:loco}: the model places high mass on G1 even on test windows whose true command is not G1, producing high-confidence wrong predictions.

\paragraph{Post-hoc temperature scaling.} The standard remedy for overconfidence is post-hoc temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration,desai2020calibration}: divide the logits by a single learned scalar $T > 0$ before the softmax. We split each fold's test positions $50 / 50$ into a calibration half (used only to fit $T$ by minimising NLL via LBFGS) and an evaluation half (used to measure post-correction ECE), with the seed-42 random split applied per head per fold. The cross-fold-aggregated fitted temperatures are $T_{\text{cmd}} = 1.34 \pm 0.25$, $T_{\text{type}} = 0.61 \pm 0.15$, $T_{\text{pt}} = 0.57 \pm 0.09$ ($T > 1$ softens an overconfident distribution; $T < 1$ sharpens an underconfident one); fold 1 fits the highest command-head temperature ($T_{\text{cmd}}^{(1)} = 1.77$, matching the single-fold value reported in earlier drafts) because fold 1 is the outlier fold with the largest covariate-shift effect (Section~\ref{sec:lim-covariate}). Across the 5 folds, the temperature-scaled command head reaches ECE $0.020 \pm 0.011$ (mean $\pm$ std) on the evaluation half --- a $3.0$~pp ECE reduction relative to the un-scaled cross-fold baseline of $0.050 \pm 0.038$. Type and parameter-type heads, already well-calibrated, gain similarly in absolute terms ($0.033 \to 0.004$ and $0.025 \to 0.002$ on ECE; \texttt{audit/calibration\_cross\_fold.json}). The single-fold fold-1 numbers reported earlier (\texttt{audit/calibration\_fold1.json}: ECE $0.122 \to 0.029$ for command) are the upper-tail behaviour from the outlier fold; the cross-fold means are the deployment-relevant numbers. Accuracy is unchanged at every fold because temperature scaling does not move the argmax. One caveat for the over-sharpened heads: on the already-well-calibrated parameter-type head, the fitted $T<1$ reduces mean ECE but \emph{raises} the worst-bin MCE on several folds (to $0.27$--$0.37$), i.e.\ it trades average calibration for a heavier worst-bin tail; a verification rule that thresholds on a single high-confidence bin should therefore apply temperature scaling to the over-confident command head but may prefer the un-scaled parameter-type head (\texttt{audit/calibration\_cross\_fold.json}). Temperature scaling adds a single fit-time scalar per head and no inference-time cost; we recommend it as the default deployment-time calibration step for the command head specifically. As a single-scalar post-hoc correction it is known to degrade under distribution shift~\cite{ovadia2019trust}, which is consistent with the fold-1 outlier behaviour and is a further reason the cross-fold mean, not the worst fold, is the deployment-relevant figure.

\subsection{Failure-Mode Analysis}
\label{sec:failure-modes}

Beyond aggregate accuracies, we classify the qualitative shape of the decoder's worst predictions to characterise \emph{how} the model fails when it fails. The worst failures are therefore predominantly structural rather than arithmetic. Section~\ref{sec:disc-failure-mode} interprets this pattern.

\subsubsection{Autoregressive Mode Collapse on Full-Window Targets}
\label{sec:disc-mode-collapse}

The qualitative-failure analysis above is computed on per-row predictions, where each sample is a single short G-code line and autoregressive decoding spans only $\sim$5--15 tokens. On full-window targets ($\sim$1{,}000--1{,}400 tokens per sample), the autoregressive decoder exhibits a qualitatively different and more severe failure mode: \emph{mode collapse to a corpus-modal token sequence}. To illustrate, we inspected the first three test samples in fold~1's full-window split and report their autoregressive (beam-width-one) decodings:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
\begin{tabular}{l p{0.78\textwidth}}
sample 1 TRUE & X NUM\_X\_1627 Y NUM\_Y\_1464 Z NUM\_Z\_0095 X NUM\_X\_1623 Y NUM\_Y\_1687 \ldots \\
sample 1 PRED & X NUM\_X\_1466 Y NUM\_Y\_1630 Z NUM\_Z\_0052 X NUM\_X\_1455 Y NUM\_Y\_1607 \ldots \\
\addlinespace
sample 2 TRUE & X NUM\_X\_1466 Y NUM\_Y\_1630 Z NUM\_Z\_0052 X NUM\_X\_1457 Y NUM\_Y\_1607 \ldots \\
sample 2 PRED & X NUM\_X\_1466 Y NUM\_Y\_1630 Z NUM\_Z\_0052 X NUM\_X\_1457 Y NUM\_Y\_1607 \ldots \\
\addlinespace
sample 3 TRUE & X NUM\_X\_1569 Y NUM\_Y\_1453 Z NUM\_Z\_0097 X NUM\_X\_1676 Y NUM\_Y\_1645 \ldots \\
sample 3 PRED & X NUM\_X\_1466 Y NUM\_Y\_1630 Z NUM\_Z\_0052 X NUM\_X\_1455 Y NUM\_Y\_1607 \ldots \\
\end{tabular}
\end{quote}
Samples 1 and 3 receive essentially the same predicted token stream as each other, despite having visibly different TRUE sequences. Sample 2's TRUE happens to coincide with the corpus-modal sequence, so it scores near-perfectly purely by accident. The decoder has converged to a canonical ``facing-operation toolpath'' and emits it autoregressively regardless of the sensor input that conditioned the encoder memory. Cross-attention into the encoder memory is effectively dropped under autoregressive generation: the decoder's own previous-token state dominates, and once on the modal trajectory the decoder stays there.

This mode collapse is the mechanism behind the autoregressive-token-accuracy drop reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}. It does not affect the categorical heads, which take their input directly from the encoder memory and do not depend on the autoregressive token state; categorical accuracies (command, axis-presence, sign, type) are identical between teacher-forced and autoregressive evaluation regimes for this reason. Figure~\ref{fig:mode_collapse_heatmap} quantifies the effect as a pairwise prediction-similarity matrix across test samples. The primary evidence for the mechanism is the output-diversity audit ($3.3\times$ over-representation of the modal pattern) and the per-operation-class breakdown; the target-length correlation (Figure~\ref{fig:length_vs_accuracy}, Pearson $r \approx -0.15$) is weak and we treat it only as corroborating, not load-bearing. Section~\ref{sec:disc-when-to-use} recommends per-row mode at deployment time on this basis.

\paragraph{Output-string diversity (partial vs.\ total collapse).} A natural quantitative test of mode collapse is the diversity of distinct output strings produced across the test set. Across the 549 test samples in the baseline 5-fold split, the autoregressive decoder produces 407 distinct predicted token-strings (74\% of inputs map to a distinct output); the corresponding ground-truth set contains 459 distinct strings (84\%). The modal predicted string appears in $9.5\%$ of predictions on average, versus a modal ground-truth string appearing in $2.9\%$ of windows --- a $3.3\times$ over-representation of the modal pattern (\texttt{audit/ar\_output\_diversity.json}). The collapse is therefore \emph{partial}: the decoder does not produce a single canonical output, but it over-concentrates on the modal facing-pattern at roughly three times the natural rate. This is consistent with the per-class breakdown above and the heatmap in Figure~\ref{fig:mode_collapse_heatmap}: within an operation-class block the predictions are mostly the same, but across classes the model does emit visibly different (though equally wrong) token strings.

\subsubsection{Positional-Metadata Ablation}
\label{sec:abl-positional}
Re-training the decoder \emph{with} positional metadata exposed (window index, total-window count, source-file hash) produces the per-metric effect sizes in Table~\ref{tab:anova}. One-way ANOVA across the 5 folds of each configuration shows a clean factorization:

\begin{itemize}
\item Categorical heads --- unaffected by positional metadata (command $F=0.02$, $p=0.89$; type $F=0.04$, $p=0.85$; param-type $F=0.01$, $p=0.93$; sign $F=0.00$, $p=0.96$, all under AR evaluation; identical p-values under TF).
\item Autoregressive heads --- token $F=4.52$, $p=0.066$, $\Delta=+7.5$~pp; sequence $F=27.18$, $p=0.0008$, $\Delta=+7.6$~pp; numeric $F=18.96$, $p=0.0024$, $\Delta=+8.0$~pp. Sequence and numeric heads are improved by positional metadata under AR evaluation at $p < 0.01$; the token-head improvement is borderline ($p=0.066$, marginal at $\alpha=0.05$). Under TF evaluation the same comparison shows no improvement on any head (token $p=0.21$, numeric $p=0.13$, sequence the sole TF-significant comparison at $p=0.0006$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Categorical heads} (command, type, parameter-type, sign, per-axis presence and sign) are robustly recoverable from sensors alone, and exposing positional metadata does not improve them under any evaluation regime. The with-shortcuts 5-fold command-accuracy mean is $0.8803 \pm 0.0564$, statistically indistinguishable from the no-shortcuts $0.8875 \pm 0.0558$ baseline. These heads are independent per-position classifiers on the encoder memory; their accuracies are identical under teacher-forced and autoregressive decoding (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\textbf{Autoregressive-token heads} (token, numeric, sequence) show a more nuanced picture that depends on the evaluation regime. Under teacher-forced eval the position metadata provides no lift and is mildly harmful ($-2.7$~pp on token, $-5.3$~pp on numeric in 5-fold means). Under autoregressive deployment-true evaluation, however, the picture reverses: position metadata produces approximately $+8$--$9$~pp on both token and numeric accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}). The mechanism is mitigation of autoregressive mode collapse: in the no-shortcuts AR setting, the decoder converges to a corpus-modal token sequence regardless of sensor input; exposing the window index gives the decoder a per-window anchor that breaks the modal trajectory on a fraction of test samples. Position metadata is therefore not a ``shortcut'' that inflates sweep-time accuracy; it is a deployment-time disambiguation signal that partially compensates for exposure bias in long-sequence autoregressive decoding. Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals for both configurations appear in Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap_ci}.

\paragraph{Multiple-comparisons correction on the drilled grid.} The per-class / per-axis / per-position ANOVA grid is run as the methodology section commits (Appendix~\ref{app:repl-stats}). The grid covers 8 ablation configurations (noise augmentation and the seven zeroed-modality ablations: accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, environmental, color, audio-RMS, electrical) against the no-shortcuts baseline, drilled across the per-class precision/recall/F1 for type, command, parameter-type, sign, and the per-digit-value / per-position numeric heads, for a total of $630$ (ablation, drilled-metric) tests (\texttt{audit/anova\_per\_class\_results.json}). Under BH-FDR adjustment ($\alpha=0.05$), $320 / 630$ tests are significant; under the more conservative Holm-Bonferroni adjustment, $72 / 630$ are significant. The bulk of the BH-FDR-significant tests concentrate on the noise-augmentation ablation (per-class command F1 drops of $0.3$--$0.7$ for G0/G1/G2/G3, all with adjusted $p < 0.05$; this is the negative-result the noise-aug section reports) and on the uniform command-head degradation shared by all seven sensor-modality ablations (consistent with the non-significant between-modality ANOVA in Section~\ref{sec:abl-sensors}). The macro-metric ANOVA results in Table~\ref{tab:anova} survive both Holm-Bonferroni and BH-FDR adjustment for the sequence-head and numeric-head AR-evaluation positives ($p < 0.01$); the token-head $p=0.066$ borderline does not survive either adjustment.

\subsubsection{Target-Mode Ablation (Per-Row vs.\ Full-Window)}
\label{sec:abl-target-mode}

The per-row target formulation (Section~\ref{sec:per-row}) leaves the decoder without a within-window row-identification signal: every row of a given window shares the same encoder memory, and the decoder must predict a specific row from that shared input. A natural alternative is full-window targeting, in which the supervisory signal is the entire multi-line G-code that fired within the window, generated autoregressively. Row identification then comes from the decoder's own output context: row $r$ is predicted conditional on rows $1\!\dots\!r-1$. The corresponding row in Table~\ref{tab:ablations} reports the resulting accuracies.

The target-mode contrast --- per-row (one G-code line per window) versus full-window (the full multi-line sequence within each window) --- isolates the contribution of autoregressive context to row-level recovery. Holding the architecture, hyperparameters, and training data fixed, the full-window formulation produces a substantial lift on the command head: command accuracy moves from per-row $0.499$ to full-window $0.888$, a +38.9~pp gain. Because the command head is an independent classifier on the encoder memory and not autoregressive in its output stream, this lift is robust across both teacher-forced and autoregressive evaluation regimes (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}); it reflects an actual change in what the decoder is able to recover, not a difference in eval condition. Section~\ref{sec:disc-failure-mode} interprets the lift mechanistically: in per-row mode all rows within a 64-second window share the same encoder memory, so the decoder cannot disambiguate which of $\sim$60 candidate rows it is asked to emit; in full-window mode the multi-line target gives every row a unique within-window position signal, restoring identifiability for the command head.

On the autoregressive-token heads (token, numeric, sequence), the per-row vs.\ full-window contrast is more nuanced. In teacher-forced evaluation the full-window formulation reports higher token and numeric accuracy than per-row, but in autoregressive evaluation the difference shrinks substantially because exposure-bias errors compound over the longer full-window sequences. We therefore report the full-window formulation as the deployment-recommended configuration only when categorical recovery is the objective; when end-to-end sequence reproduction is the objective, per-row mode is comparable in autoregressive performance and has the practical advantage of much shorter, parallelisable per-row inference.

\subsection{RQ3: Sensor-Modality Ablation}
\label{sec:abl-sensors}
For each of seven sensor modality groups (accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer, environmental, color, audio-RMS, electrical) we zero the corresponding sensor channels at the encoder input, then evaluate the decoder. The encoder is not retrained; the zeroing happens only at decoder-inference time, which isolates the marginal contribution of each modality \emph{conditional on the encoder's existing weight allocation}. We emphasise that this is a \emph{lower bound} on the value of each modality: an encoder retrained without a given modality could re-allocate capacity onto the remaining channels and would be the appropriate test of modality-redundancy at the system level; the inference-time zeroing reported here tests only what the existing encoder happens to extract from that modality. All seven modality groups are evaluated at the full 5-fold ($n=5$) protocol.

\paragraph{The finding: uniform collapse, not modality differentiation.} The full 5-fold ablation is now complete for all seven modality groups. At the inference-time-zeroing test every modality is critical to the command head and the magnitude is statistically uniform: zeroing any single modality drops command accuracy from the full-modality baseline of $0.888 \pm 0.056$ to the $0.449$--$0.484$ band (accelerometer $0.484$, environmental $0.474$, magnetometer $0.465$, gyroscope $0.462$, rms $0.460$, color $0.457$, electrical $0.449$; all $\pm 0.05$--$0.11$ across folds), a uniform $-40$ to $-44$~pp collapse. A one-way ANOVA across the seven zeroed-modality groups is \emph{not} significant ($F=0.07$, $p=0.998$; \texttt{audit/sensor\_anova\_7modality.json}) --- the seven modalities are statistically indistinguishable from one another --- while every modality \emph{versus} the full-modality baseline \emph{is} significant under a Welch test ($p<0.001$ in all seven cases, $t$ from $-7.1$ to $-11.5$). \textbf{No single modality is more deletable than another at this test;} the test confirms joint necessity but cannot rank importance. Token, type, and parameter-type heads are far more robust: token drops $\sim 4$~pp, type $<1$~pp, and parameter-type $\sim 1.5$~pp across every ablation. Table~\ref{tab:sensor_ablation} reports the full 5-fold numbers and Figure~\ref{fig:sensor_ablation} shows the bar chart.

\input{../tables/sensor_ablation.tex}

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The encoder's learned modality-gating routes essential command-discrimination signal through every modality at once; when any one is zeroed at inference the gating mechanism degenerates to a representation that is informative enough for the coarse (type, param-type) heads but not for the finer command head. This means the inference-time zeroing test cannot rank the relative importance of modalities --- it can only confirm that they are all jointly important. The genuine modality-importance ranking, and the answer to ``which sensors can a production deployment afford to omit'', requires an encoder retrained from scratch without each modality. That experiment is the planned encoder-retraining follow-up (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}); pending it, the deployment claim we can defensibly make is that the \emph{full multi-modal sensor stack} is required for the categorical-recovery story this paper reports.

\paragraph{Comparison with encoder-paper modality-importance.} The encoder paper~\cite{encoder_paper} reported gyroscope, color, and electrical as the highest-utility modalities for operation classification under a per-modality SHAP-style attribution at the encoder input. The decoder-side ablation reported here does not reproduce that ranking because it operates on a frozen, fully-trained encoder rather than on the marginal information content of each modality; the two analyses answer different questions. The decoder ablation says ``every modality is jointly necessary at inference under the existing encoder''; the encoder paper says ``these modalities carried the most operation-class signal during encoder training.'' The non-significant $7$-group ANOVA ($p=0.998$) is the formal statement of this: across the full 5-fold sweep the encoder-paper's ``most-important'' modalities (gyroscope, color) are not separable from the rest at the decoder-ablation level. The reconciliation is the retrained-encoder ablation referenced above: if encoder-retraining-without-modality-X still gives the decoder reasonable command accuracy, then modality X is deployment-deletable; otherwise modality X is genuinely load-bearing. That experiment, not inference-time zeroing, is the correct test of the deployment cost-reduction question and is the next planned contribution (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

\subsubsection{Sequence-Classifier (Pattern-Aware) Ablation}
\label{sec:abl-pattern}
A sequence-classifier head trained jointly with the legacy-token head predicts \emph{which whole G-code line} the model is reconstructing, then biases the token-level distribution toward that line's tokens. The V8 pattern-aware fold-1 pilot (\texttt{outputs/decoder20260511/checkpoints/per\_row\_pattern\_aware/fold\_1/}) achieves command $0.536$, token $0.694$, numeric $0.392$, parameter-type $0.952$, type $0.975$. This is a \emph{negative result} on V8: command accuracy ($0.536$) is below the fold-1 baseline ($0.776$) and token accuracy ($0.694$) is below the fold-1 baseline ($0.747$). The earlier V7-legacy single-fold approximation had suggested a small ($\sim$2~pp) positive effect; the V8 result does not reproduce it. We interpret the regression as the sequence-classifier prior over-committing to a whole-line hypothesis early in decoding, which compounds the autoregressive exposure-bias problem (Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}) rather than mitigating it. The pattern-aware head is therefore not recommended on this corpus; we report it as a clean negative result and do not promote it to a 5-fold sweep.

\subsubsection{Vocabulary Precision Ablation}
\label{sec:abl-vocab}
A 2-digit precision vocabulary (\texttt{NUM\_X\_15} bucketing rather than \texttt{NUM\_X\_1492}) reduces the numeric output cardinality by approximately $5\times$ (vocabulary size 451 vs.\ 2{,}418). The hypothesis was that a coarser numeric target would be easier to predict and would lift numeric accuracy at the cost of precision. Trained on fold 1 with otherwise identical configuration (per-row targets, $300$ epochs), the 2-digit decoder achieves: token $0.659$, sequence $0.012$, command $0.290$, numeric $0.305$, parameter-type $0.974$, type $0.953$ (\texttt{outputs/decoder20260511/checkpoints/per\_row\_vocab2digit/fold\_1/}). This is a \emph{negative result}: the coarser numeric bucketing does not improve numeric recovery (numeric accuracy $0.305$ does not exceed the fine-vocabulary fold-1 numeric accuracy), and command accuracy is also lower, while parameter-type holds at $0.974$. The structured-output decomposition (type, parameter-type) is robust to vocabulary coarseness, but coarsening the numeric vocabulary does not yield the hypothesised numeric-accuracy gain --- the bottleneck is the autoregressive sequence head (Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}), not the numeric output cardinality, so reducing the cardinality does not help. Exact-line reconstruction additionally depends on the fine-grained numeric vocabulary, so the 2-digit configuration is dominated on every axis and is not recommended.

\subsubsection{Window and Stride}
\label{sec:abl-window-stride}
A window/stride ablation requires a sensor encoder retrained for each $(\text{window}, \text{stride})$ pair, since the frozen encoder consumes a fixed-shape input. We ran this properly: for each cell the preprocessing, the encoder (encoder-paper recipe), and the decoder were all retrained from scratch at that window and stride (\texttt{outputs/decoder20260511/checkpoints/window\_stride\_v2/}; \texttt{audit/window\_stride\_v2\_summary.json}). The headline configuration ($\text{window}=256, \text{stride}=64$, command $0.888$) was inherited from the encoder paper's nine-class classification optimum~\cite{encoder_paper}; the sweep tests whether that inheritance is also optimal for instruction-level decoding.

The seven retrained-encoder cells (fold 1) give command accuracies of $w64{:}s16 \to 0.600$, $w128{:}s32 \to 0.513$, $w512{:}s128 \to 0.488$, $w128{:}s16 \to 0.468$, $w64{:}s32 \to 0.457$, $w128{:}s64 \to 0.433$, $w256{:}s128 \to 0.345$ (\texttt{audit/window\_stride\_v2\_summary.json}). \emph{Every} swept configuration is well below the inherited $w256{:}s64$ headline of $0.888$ --- the best swept cell ($w64{:}s16$, $0.600$) is still $29$~pp short, and the longest window tested ($w512{:}s128$, $0.488$) does \emph{not} recover the headline either, so the hypothesis that a longer window would help is also ruled out. Token ($\sim$0.74) and numeric ($\sim$0.46) likewise sit below the headline $0.781 / 0.585$, while type and parameter-type stay near-saturated ($\sim$0.98) at every cell. Two further observations: (i) the only window-matched comparison, $w256{:}s128$ ($0.345$) versus the $w256{:}s64$ headline ($0.888$), isolates stride as itself load-bearing --- halving the stride density at fixed window size more than doubles command accuracy, because the denser stride yields more training windows per file; (ii) within the swept set there is no monotone window-length trend, so the result is best read as ``no $(\text{window}, \text{stride})$ in the swept region matches the inherited configuration,'' a clean negative control that rules out both the ``shorter window localises the per-row signal better'' and the ``longer window gives more context'' hypotheses. Each cell retrains the encoder from scratch, so the sweep is fold-1 per cell by design; a full 5-fold confirmation is deferred to the encoder-retraining study (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}), but the cross-cell spread ($0.345$--$0.600$) is large relative to the per-cell fold variation seen elsewhere and the gap to the headline is far larger than any plausible fold noise.

\subsection{RQ4: Cross-Class Generalization (Leave-One-Class-Out)}
\label{sec:loco}

For each of the nine operation classes (\texttt{adaptive}, \texttt{adaptive150025}, \texttt{face}, \texttt{face150025}, \texttt{pocket}, \texttt{pocket150025}, \texttt{damageadaptive}, \texttt{damageface}, \texttt{damagepocket}), we train the decoder without that class in the training partition and evaluate on its held-out test samples. This stress-tests the decoder's ability to generalize across machining operations rather than memorize per-class shortcuts.

Averaged across all nine LOCO holdouts: token accuracy $0.501 \pm 0.093$ (95\% CI $[0.435, 0.555]$), sequence accuracy $0.001 \pm 0.002$, command accuracy $0.213 \pm 0.191$ (95\% CI $[0.103, 0.353]$; median $0.21$), numeric accuracy $0.152 \pm 0.133$ (95\% CI $[0.068, 0.241]$) (one fold per holdout, fold-1 file split; bootstrap 10{,}000 percentile-method resamples on the nine per-holdout means; \texttt{audit/loco\_bootstrap\_ci.json}). Type and parameter-type accuracy are markedly more robust: type $0.883 \pm 0.061$ (95\% CI $[0.844, 0.922]$) and parameter-type $0.945 \pm 0.036$ (95\% CI $[0.921, 0.968]$) across the nine holdouts. Relative to the in-distribution 5-fold baseline ($0.781 / 0.026 / 0.888 / 0.585$), the LOCO setting incurs a $\sim$$28$~pp drop on token accuracy, a $\sim$$3$~pp drop on sequence accuracy, a $\sim$$67$~pp drop on command accuracy (mean), and a $\sim$$43$~pp drop on numeric accuracy. The degradation is statistically significant on every recoverable metric: a Welch two-sample test between the nine LOCO holdouts and the five in-distribution folds gives command $t=-9.24$, $p<10^{-5}$; token $t=-8.14$, $p<10^{-4}$; numeric $t=-8.68$, $p<10^{-4}$, with the nonparametric Mann--Whitney $U$ confirming each at $p \le 0.003$ (\texttt{audit/loco\_significance.json}). The cross-class generalisation gap is therefore not attributable to fold-level sampling noise.

\paragraph{Per-holdout variance and the \texttt{damagepocket} outlier.} The nine per-holdout command accuracies span $[0.000, 0.678]$ with $\sigma = 0.191$. Eight of the nine holdouts cluster in $[0.000, 0.273]$ ($\sim$ in line with the mode-collapse story below); one holdout, \texttt{damagepocket}, reaches command accuracy $0.678$, token $0.548$, and numeric $0.402$. The structural reason is that the \texttt{damagepocket} test class shares most of its motion vocabulary (predominantly G1 linear cuts with modest G3 finishing arcs) with the \texttt{pocket} and \texttt{pocket150025} variants retained in training; the model transfers its in-distribution pocket-pattern command discrimination to the held-out damaged-pocket samples with only modest degradation. By contrast, \texttt{damageadaptive} (command $0.000$) and \texttt{damageface} (command $0.094$) do not benefit similarly from their parent operations because the adaptive and face motion patterns differ more strongly between damaged and non-damaged variants than the pocket variants do. The lesson is that cross-class generalisation is not uniformly absent: it succeeds when the held-out class shares motion vocabulary with retained training classes, and collapses otherwise. The median across the nine holdouts ($0.21$) better reflects the typical cross-class behaviour than the mean.

\paragraph{Per-class LOCO command F1.} The typical-holdout cross-class collapse is concentrated across every command class, not on a subset. Averaged across all 9 holdouts (\texttt{audit/loco\_per\_class\_9holdouts.json}), the per-class F1 on the command head is G0 $0.084 \pm 0.136$, G1 $0.243 \pm 0.277$ (the modal class; large variance reflects the \texttt{damagepocket} outlier), G2 $0.044 \pm 0.107$, G3 $0.083 \pm 0.134$, and G53 / M30 at $0.000$ (these two classes have $n \le 5$ test support per holdout and the model never assigns them under LOCO). The pattern is consistent with the mode-collapse mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}): under a held-out operation class whose motion vocabulary is not represented in training, the model defaults to its training-corpus modal output (G1), which produces above-chance F1 only on G1 itself and near-zero F1 on the other classes. The in-distribution 5-fold command accuracy therefore reflects the model's ability to discriminate \emph{within the in-distribution operation set}; cross-operation-class command discrimination is supported only when the held-out class shares motion vocabulary with retained training classes (the \texttt{damagepocket} regime above). The coarsest classification heads (type at 4 classes; parameter-type at 10 classes, of which only X/Y/Z/R/F appear with non-trivial support) survive LOCO with comparatively small degradation ($-9$~pp and $-5$~pp respectively), suggesting that the encoder representation preserves gross instruction structure (``this row is a NUMERIC token at an X coordinate'') even when the specific motion class is novel; the finer-grained heads (command, numeric digits) require the in-distribution operation pattern. This is the explicit open-vocabulary generalisation result the verbatim-overlap disclosure in Section~\ref{sec:lim-dataset} points to: command identity and digit values are recovered under the closed-vocabulary 5-fold protocol, but the cross-class generalisation gap is large, and the deployment-realistic claim for an instruction-level monitor is bounded by the LOCO numbers, not by the 5-fold headline. Figure~\ref{fig:loco_per_holdout} shows the per-holdout breakdown across all nine LOCO holdouts and all six heads, with the in-distribution baseline as a reference; the \texttt{damagepocket} outlier and the LOCO-robust type / parameter-type heads are directly visible.

\subsection{Noise Augmentation}
\label{sec:noise-aug}

Adding Gaussian sensor noise ($\sigma=0.02$) plus per-feature dropout and per-token masking during training substantially \emph{degrades} command-head accuracy on this corpus while leaving the autoregressive-token heads roughly intact. 5-fold averages on per-row targets under teacher-forced evaluation: token $0.732 \pm 0.011$, sequence $0.006 \pm 0.001$, command $0.331 \pm 0.076$, parameter-type $0.978 \pm 0.002$, numeric $0.448 \pm 0.010$. Relative to the no-augmentation per-row baseline (token $\sim$0.74, command $\sim$0.86, numeric $\sim$0.45), the noise-augmentation regime preserves token and numeric accuracy but drops command accuracy by approximately 53~pp. The interpretation is consistent with regularisation pushing the command head toward predicting the modal class (G1) rather than discriminating G0/G2/G3: noise injection makes the marginal command-discrimination signal in the encoder memory less reliable from the decoder's perspective, so the model defaults to the high-prior class. We therefore report noise augmentation as a clean negative result on this corpus and do not recommend it as a deployment configuration. Noise injection may still be useful in regimes with a wider per-class corpus or under a different loss-weighting that decouples token-level and command-level regularisation; those configurations are left to future work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Three Structural Tiers of G-Code Recoverability}
\label{sec:disc-recoverability}

The headline finding is a clean factorization of G-code recoverability into three structural tiers:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Categorical structure} (command identity, parameter-type identity) is recovered with $0.8875 \pm 0.0558$ and $0.9931 \pm 0.0036$ accuracy from sensors alone, no positional metadata. The ANOVA across the metadata ablation confirms that exposing positional features does not significantly improve these metrics. This is the strongest finding: \emph{the sensor pathway carries enough information to determine which class of motion is happening and which axes are involved}, independently of where in the file the window came from.

\item \textbf{Motion sign} is recovered with axis-asymmetric fidelity. Per-axis sign accuracies (Table~\ref{tab:per_axis}, 5-fold mean) are Y-sign $0.997 \pm 0.006$, X-sign $0.942 \pm 0.033$, and Z-sign $0.938 \pm 0.023$. Pooled motion-sign accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:headline}) is $0.989 \pm 0.006$. The X- and Z-axis dips suggest that, on this machine geometry, the sensor signatures for $+X/-X$ and $+Z/-Z$ motions are less distinguishable than those for $+Y/-Y$. This may reflect the spatial layout of the sensor boards relative to the workpiece origin or the workpiece's symmetric geometry along the X axis. A confirmatory experiment that rotates the workpiece would isolate which effect dominates.

\item \textbf{Numeric value precision} is bounded by an information-theoretic limit. The metadata-versus-sensor split (Section~\ref{sec:abl-positional}) shows that withholding positional features removes $\sim$8~pp of numeric accuracy. The per-digit decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:numeric-decomposition}) shows that the model's loss is concentrated at the middle digit positions --- the positions that encode fine-grained coordinate value, after the magnitude is known. A 64-second sensor window does not uniquely determine where a tool is in absolute machine coordinates; only relative motion is locally observable. Recovering absolute coordinates therefore depends on either positional context (which we deliberately withhold) or longer-range autoregressive context (which the per-row formulation does not provide).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Failure Modes are Structural, not Arithmetic}
\label{sec:disc-failure-mode}

The failure-mode analysis (Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes}) shows that the decoder's worst-edit-distance failures are dominated by command-identity confusion: dropped, swapped, or hallucinated G-commands. Pure value-only errors --- correct structure with wrong numeric values --- are essentially absent from the worst-failure list. This is informative for two reasons.

First, it locates the practical bottleneck. In the per-row formulation, the decoder must produce a stereotyped output pattern per window because every row of the window shares the same encoder memory. When rows within a window genuinely differ in their G-command (a face-cut row alternating with a Z-plunge row, for example), the per-row decoder collapses to the most-common pattern and emits the same prediction for several different test rows. This is exactly the within-window collapse predicted by the per-row formulation's design (Section~\ref{sec:per-row}).

Second, it predicts where full-window targeting should help. The row-level autoregressive context in full-window mode gives the decoder access to the previous row's command when generating the current row, eliminating the structural ambiguity. The full-window vs.\ per-row comparison (Section~\ref{sec:abl-target-mode}) tests this prediction directly.

\subsection{Sensor-Modality Importance and Deployment Implications}
\label{sec:disc-modality}

The sensor-modality ablation does not differentiate modality importance: removing any single modality at inference time drops command accuracy by a uniform $40$--$44$~pp across all seven modalities tested, with a non-significant between-modality ANOVA ($F=0.07$, $p=0.998$) and every modality-vs-baseline contrast significant at $p<0.001$ (Section~\ref{sec:abl-sensors}). The result tells us that the encoder's learned modality-gating routes essential command-discrimination signal through every modality jointly; it does not tell us which modalities a deployment can omit. Token and parameter-type accuracy are far more robust ($-2$--$3$~pp and $\le 1$~pp), indicating that the gross instruction structure is preserved even when one modality is missing but the finer command discrimination is lost. The genuine modality-importance ranking, and therefore the cost-optimisation question of which sensors to retain in a deployment, requires a per-modality encoder-retraining study and is part of the planned follow-up (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

For a practical deployment, this maps onto a clear sensor-cost trade-off: a monitoring system with only the load-bearing sensor groups would substantially reduce hardware cost while retaining most of headline accuracy. For applications such as tamper detection, where the goal is to flag mismatches between commanded and observed instruction streams~\cite{kimmell2025cnc,nist2023sp80082,nist2024fmnet}, this trade-off is favourable.

\subsection{Comparison with Side-Channel Recovery in Additive Manufacturing}
\label{sec:disc-comparison-am}

Prior work on additive-manufacturing side-channels~\cite{alfaruque2016acoustic,song2016my,chhetri2016kcad,jamarani2024screg,chattopadhyay2025video} reports successful geometric reconstruction of full prints from acoustic, magnetic, or video signals. The present results recover comparably high \emph{categorical} fidelity (command and axis identity), but our \emph{geometric} fidelity --- the fine-grained X/Y/Z coordinate value --- is markedly lower than the additive-manufacturing reports. Three factors plausibly account for the gap:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Signal environment.} Additive manufacturing operates in a low-noise environment with single-axis-at-a-time deposition. CNC milling produces simultaneous multi-axis interpolation under high cutting forces, generating overlapping vibration, audio, and current signatures that obscure individual instructions.
\item \textbf{Sensor placement.} The additive-manufacturing studies place sensors freely; CNC sensor placement must respect chip ejection and cutting forces, restricting placement to the machine frame, bed, and spindle.
\item \textbf{Sampling rate.} The present sensor stack samples at 4~Hz, sufficient for operation-class boundaries but insufficient for the sub-second motions that distinguish individual rapid moves at high feed rates. A future deployment with higher-rate sensors (Section~\ref{sec:future}) is expected to recover finer-grained geometric structure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When to Use This Decoder vs.\ Alternative Approaches}
\label{sec:disc-when-to-use}

The decoder is best suited to one of three deployment patterns:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Process verification.} Run the decoder in parallel with the controller and flag discrepancies between commanded G-code and the per-row prediction. Command-level disagreements (e.g.\ G0 commanded, G1 predicted; or X commanded, Y predicted) are high-precision alerts.
\item \textbf{Post-hoc forensic analysis.} On suspect runs, the decoded G-code provides an auditable record independent of the controller log. Numeric-value precision is too low for forensic reconstruction of coordinates, but command-level analysis is reliable.
\item \textbf{Anomaly localisation.} Operations where command-level predictions deviate from the controller's commanded program point to specific G-code lines worth investigating, complementing session-level anomaly classifiers~\cite{coelho2024anomaly,kimmell2025cnc}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Deployment-time target mode: prefer per-row.} In autoregressive deployment the per-row target mode (each window predicts the single G-code line that fired within it) is preferable to the full-window mode used for training-time analysis. Per-row predictions are short (5--15 tokens), independent across rows, and consequently robust to the autoregressive mode-collapse that affects full-window long-sequence generation (Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}). Categorical-head accuracy --- the primary signal for the three deployment patterns above --- is unaffected by target mode because the categorical heads are independent classifiers on the encoder memory rather than autoregressive token predictors. The full-window mode remains the natural training-time configuration for studying within-window row-disambiguation, but it should not be used at deployment time when the goal is reliable per-instruction recovery.

Conversely, the decoder is \emph{not} a stand-alone replacement for the controller log (no exact-line reproduction) and is not yet suitable for sub-millimetre tool-path verification (numeric precision is insufficient). The combination of the present decoder with the encoder paper's operation classifier provides a layered verification stack: the encoder flags session-level operation-class anomalies, and the decoder localises them to specific G-code lines.

\paragraph{Differentiation from file-level program audit.} A natural objection to a sensor-driven verification monitor is that a cryptographic hash or signature on the G-code file is cheaper, more precise, and detects program-level tampering without any sensor overhead. The complementary value of the sensor-based decoder is that it is \emph{physics-grounded}: it observes what the machine actually did, not what the controller claims it executed. The threat classes the decoder catches that a file-level audit cannot include (i) a controller that loads the audited program but executes a substituted one (firmware compromise, RAM tampering, controller-side runtime substitution); (ii) a controller that selectively skips, duplicates, or stalls lines while reporting nominal execution; and (iii) operations on the wrong workpiece, fixturing, or tool where the program is unchanged but the physics is not what was commanded. A file-hash audit is the cheaper first line of defence; the sensor decoder is the second, physics-of-record line that runs in parallel and catches divergences invisible at the file level. Both are needed for a defence-in-depth verification stack on machines where the controller is not in the trust boundary.

\paragraph{Recommended deployment configuration.} Putting the deployment-relevant findings together (Sections~\ref{sec:disc-when-to-use}, \ref{sec:disc-threat-model}, \ref{sec:calibration}, and the aggregation analysis below):

\subsection{Threat Model and Cybersecurity Positioning}
\label{sec:disc-threat-model}

The Introduction motivated the decoder in part by reference to NIST SP 800-82~\cite{nist2023sp80082} and instruction-stream integrity in operational-technology systems. Following the manufacturing-cybersecurity literature~\cite{sturm2014cyber,yampolskiy2018security,kimmell2025cnc,zonouz2014plc,goh2017dataset}, we formalise the threat model.

\paragraph{Trusted elements.} We assume the multi-modal sensor stack is trustworthy: sensors are physically secured, the sampling pipeline is integrity-protected, and the recorded sensor stream is faithful to the physical machine state. We further assume the decoder model and the verification host are not co-located with the controller and are not compromised. These assumptions are realistic for a process-monitoring deployment where sensors and analysis live outside the CNC controller's trust boundary, but limit the threat model's reach: an adversary with physical access to the sensor stack defeats this verification.

\paragraph{Untrusted elements.} The CNC controller, its firmware, and any G-code dispatch path between controller and machine are untrusted. The adversary may have read/write access to the controller's program memory, may inject substituted G-code, may modify cutting parameters mid-execution, or may replay an earlier benign program while a different program is intended.

\paragraph{Attack classes considered.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{G-code substitution}: the adversary replaces an intended G-code line with a different one (e.g., \texttt{G1 X1.5} becomes \texttt{G1 X2.5}). The substituted instruction may differ in command identity, axis, sign, or numeric value.
\item \textbf{Parameter tampering}: a benign G-code program is preserved structurally but parameters are altered (feed rate, spindle speed, depth of cut).
\item \textbf{Replay}: a previously-executed valid program is replayed while a different program was commanded.
\item \textbf{Skip/duplicate}: individual lines are omitted or repeated.
\item \textbf{Upstream CAM / post-processor compromise}: the toolpath is corrupted \emph{before} the operator-signed G-code file is produced --- a malicious or buggy CAM post-processor, or a supply-chain modification of the design-to-G-code pipeline~\cite{belikovetsky2017drowned,wells2014cyber}. Here the file the operator audits is itself already wrong, so a file-level hash would pass.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope boundary for the upstream adversary.} The decoder's value against the upstream CAM/post-processor adversary is fundamentally limited by the closed-vocabulary regime characterised in Section~\ref{sec:lim-dataset}. If the substituted toolpath stays within the in-vocabulary G-code-line distribution the model was trained on, the decoder's prediction will agree with the (already-malicious) controller stream and the tamper is invisible to it; the decoder catches an upstream substitution only when the malicious toolpath drives the machine into a physically distinguishable, in-distribution-recognisable regime. This is a material blind spot and we state it plainly: the present system is a controller-side-substitution and execution-integrity monitor, not an upstream-design-integrity monitor. Detecting upstream CAM compromise requires either a trusted design-to-file provenance chain (orthogonal to this work) or open-vocabulary recovery strong enough to flag an unfamiliar-but-valid toolpath --- which the LOCO results (Section~\ref{sec:loco}) show the present model does not have.

\paragraph{Detection capability by attack class.} Based on the per-field recoverability characterisation reported in this paper:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Command-identity substitution} (G0 $\to$ G1, G2 $\to$ G3): \emph{detectable.} Command-accuracy at $0.8875 \pm 0.0558$ indicates the decoder reliably recovers command identity from the sensor pathway. A substituted command produces a controller-log vs.\ decoder disagreement at the row level.
\item \textbf{Axis-identity substitution} (X $\to$ Y): \emph{detectable.} Parameter-type accuracy at $0.9931 \pm 0.0036$ and per-axis presence in the 0.87--0.99 range support detection of axis swaps.
\item \textbf{Motion-sign substitution} (X+ $\to$ X-): \emph{partially detectable.} Per-axis sign accuracies (Table~\ref{tab:per_axis}, 5-fold mean) are Y $0.997$, X $0.942$, Z $0.938$. The asymmetry across axes means sign-substitution attacks are easier to catch on Y than on X or Z.
\item \textbf{Numeric-value tampering} (X1.5 $\to$ X1.6): \emph{not reliably detectable on the present sensor stack at 4~Hz.} Numeric-value precision is the bound established by the metadata-versus-sensor ablation. Numeric substitutions within the within-window value range will produce decoder predictions consistent with the substituted value.
\item \textbf{Feed-rate / spindle-speed tampering}: \emph{not evaluable on the present corpus.} The DOE-driven dataset (Appendix~\ref{app:doe}) is designed to populate these fields.
\item \textbf{Replay}: \emph{detectable indirectly.} If the replayed program produces a sensor signature inconsistent with the new operating conditions (different material, different tool wear state), the decoder will produce an instruction stream consistent with the actual physics, which will disagree with the replayed controller log.
\item \textbf{Skip/duplicate at per-row granularity}: \emph{not directly addressed by the current decoder.} Per-row prediction in this paper assumes one row per query; detecting omitted rows requires temporal alignment between sensor windows and controller log, which is operationally feasible but is not benchmarked here.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{First-pass tamper-injection operating points.} To quantify the tamper-detection guarantee implied by the per-field recoverability characterisation, we ran a synthetic tamper-injection audit on the 5-fold full-window predictions under autoregressive deployment-true decoding with the FSM grammar layer enabled (Section~\ref{sec:grammar}). For each held-out sample we (i) form the honest pair $(g_{\text{true}}, g_{\text{pred}})$ and (ii) mutate $g_{\text{true}}$ in one of three ways --- command swap, motion-sign flip on the first signed axis, and feed-rate $\pm 10\times$ edit --- to form a tampered pair. The detector flags a tamper when the relevant token-class (command, sign, F-value) in $g_{\text{pred}}$ differs from the claimed value, with thresholds chosen to match the field's own confidence profile (exact match for command and sign, $>50\%$ relative deviation for F). The operating points below are computed on the 5-fold-pooled $\sim$549 applicable test samples per attack class, reported separately for the baseline (no positional metadata) and the with-positional-metadata configurations (\texttt{audit/threat\_model\_tamper\_AR\_5fold\_FSM.json} and \texttt{audit/threat\_model\_tamper\_AR\_5fold\_FSM\_shortcuts.json}):
\begin{itemize}
\item Command-swap (e.g., G0$\to$G1): \textbf{baseline} FPR $0.299$, TPR $0.921$; \textbf{with metadata} FPR $0.222$, TPR $0.948$.
\item Sign-flip (e.g., X$+ \to$ X$-$): \textbf{baseline} FPR $0.350$, TPR $0.703$; \textbf{with metadata} FPR $0.244$, TPR $0.798$.
\item Feed-rate $\pm 10\times$ edit: \textbf{baseline} FPR $0.0055$, TPR $0.216$; \textbf{with metadata} FPR $0.0146$, TPR $0.532$.
\end{itemize}
Adding positional metadata strictly improves tamper detection on every attack class: command-swap TPR moves from $0.92$ to $0.95$, sign-flip from $0.70$ to $0.80$, and feed-rate from $0.22$ to $0.53$ (a $\sim$$2.5\times$ improvement). Across the swept detection threshold, command-swap reaches AUC $0.80$/$0.86$ (baseline/with-shortcuts), while sign-flip and feed-rate detectors are near-chance (AUC $\sim$0.50) because autoregressive mode collapse strips per-axis-sign and per-feed-rate information; the ROC-space operating points are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tamper_roc_scatter}. This is the security counterpart to the mode-collapse-mitigation finding in Section~\ref{sec:abl-positional}: position metadata gives the autoregressive decoder a per-window anchor that breaks the corpus-modal-output trajectory, producing predictions that are window-specific and therefore informative enough to discriminate tampered from honest controller logs. For security-oriented deployments we recommend the with-metadata configuration; for use cases where positional metadata is unavailable or where a clean ``sensors-only'' baseline is needed, the no-metadata configuration is the conservative choice.

\paragraph{False-alarm cost.} The baseline command-swap operating point is TPR $0.92$ at FPR $0.30$ (with-metadata: TPR $0.95$ at FPR $0.22$); the sign-flip operating point is TPR $0.70$/$0.80$ at FPR $0.35$/$0.24$. On a production CNC executing tens of thousands of G-code lines per shift, an FPR in the $0.2$--$0.35$ range translates to thousands of nuisance alerts per operator per day and is not viable for a row-level verification monitor without further work. Three practical remedies recover deployable operating points from these numbers: \emph{(a) aggregation} over a sliding window of $K$ adjacent rows; \emph{(b) operating-point selection at a fixed low FPR (e.g., $0.01$)} on the ROC operating points in Figure~\ref{fig:tamper_roc_scatter} yields a much lower TPR but a deployable false-alarm budget; \emph{(c) temperature-scaled calibration} (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}) of the command head reduces ECE from $0.122$ to $0.029$ and tightens the high-confidence threshold band, which is the input the detector consumes. The operating points reported above are the threshold-matched first-pass numbers without these enhancements.

\paragraph{Aggregation simulation: deployable operating points at K-row windows.} To quantify remedy (a), we Monte-Carlo simulated $K$-row aggregation of the per-row alert under three rules (any-of-K, all-of-K, majority-of-K) at $K \in \{1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50\}$ on $549$ honest and $549$ tampered samples per attack class (\texttt{audit/tamper\_aggregation\_sweep.json}; row-independence assumption noted; Figure~\ref{fig:tamper_aggregation_curves}). The majority-of-K rule recovers deployable operating points on the command-swap detector: with-shortcuts at $K=10$ reaches \textbf{TPR $1.00$ at FPR $0.037$}; with-shortcuts at $K=5$ reaches TPR $1.00$ at FPR $0.10$; baseline (no metadata) at $K=10$ reaches TPR $1.00$ at FPR $0.056$. The all-of-K rule trades a lower TPR for an even tighter FPR: with-shortcuts at $K=5$ reaches TPR $0.75$ at FPR $0.00$. Sign-flip detection benefits less from aggregation because the per-row alert is closer to chance: even at $K=10$ majority, FPR drops to $0.04$ but TPR is held at $0.96$ only under the with-shortcuts configuration. Feed-rate aggregation cannot recover useful operating points because the per-row TPR is too low to compound: with-shortcuts $K=3$ majority reaches TPR $0.58$ at FPR $0.00$, the best operating point on that attack class. \emph{Caveat:} the simulation assumes row-independence; in practice, alerts are correlated within a window because the underlying tampered region typically spans many adjacent rows.

\paragraph{Correlated-alert sensitivity.} To gauge how much the independence assumption inflates the FPR gain we ran a first-order Markov-chain variant (\texttt{audit/tamper\_aggregation\_correlated.json}) with autocorrelation $\rho \in \{0.0, 0.3, 0.6\}$ matched to plausible deployment regimes. For command-swap at $K=10$ majority with positional metadata, FPR rises from $0.012$ at $\rho=0$ to $0.070$ at $\rho=0.3$ to $0.146$ at $\rho=0.6$, while TPR holds at $1.00$ / $0.96$ / $0.94$ respectively. The qualitative deployable-operating-point claim survives at moderate correlation ($\rho=0.3$ keeps with-shortcuts FPR below $0.10$ at near-saturated TPR) but the independence-simulation FPR of $0.04$ is a best case; realistic deployment FPR is more likely in the $0.05$--$0.15$ range, depending on the autocorrelation of the underlying tamper-injection trace. The full $(K, \rho, \text{rule})$ sweep is in the audit JSON. This refines the recommendation to: deploy with-metadata at $K \ge 10$ majority-vote, monitor the empirical FPR on representative honest traces, and budget for $5$--$15$\% nuisance alerts depending on alert autocorrelation. Figure~\ref{fig:tamper_roc_scatter} shows the same operating points in ROC space, with the high-correlation reference curve included.

The feed-rate detector's low FPR ($0.005$--$0.015$) is already deployable but its TPR ($0.22$--$0.53$) is the bound of interest; closing this gap is part of the DOE follow-up (Appendix~\ref{app:doe}).

\paragraph{Limitations of the verification.} The decoder cannot detect attacks whose physical effect is indistinguishable from a benign program at 4~Hz sample rate. Sub-second motion details (rapid clearance moves, small finishing passes) may be temporally aliased. An adversary aware of the verification scheme could in principle craft substitutions that preserve the recoverable fields (command identity, axis presence, sign) while corrupting the un-recovered fields (numeric value, feed rate). Mitigations include higher sample rate, additional sensor modalities (force, AE), and the encoder-retraining direction outlined in Section~\ref{sec:future}. The DOE-driven dataset is the path to widening the recoverable field set and tightening the detection guarantee.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\subsection{Dataset Limitations}
\label{sec:lim-dataset}

\paragraph{Continuous-parameter coverage.} The most consequential dataset limitation is the near-absence of variation in feed rate (F), spindle speed (S), and arc parameters (I/J). The data-coverage table (Table~\ref{tab:data_coverage}) reports each field's positive support: F appears in $<\!1\%$ of per-row samples; S is essentially absent from both modes; I and J never appear (arcs use R notation only). The accuracy numbers for these fields in Table~\ref{tab:per_axis} are therefore artefacts of their near-zero baseline support, not evidence of sensor-driven recovery. A designed-experiment data-collection campaign described in Appendix~\ref{app:doe} systematically varies feed rate, spindle speed, depth of cut, and material across 188 distinct runs and is the planned data-collection follow-up that targets these gaps (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

\paragraph{Material variety.} All recorded runs use a single workpiece material on a single CNC platform (a Bantam Tools desktop mill). Material-class generalisation cannot be tested on the present corpus. The Appendix~\ref{app:doe} DOE includes aluminum, steel, and Delrin to address this gap.

\paragraph{Label set scale.} The corpus contains approximately 214 distinct G-code lines per fold, with G1 strongly dominant. Per-class metrics are macro-averaged over a long tail; results for G3, M30, and arc parameters would benefit from substantially more diverse data.

\paragraph{Verbatim train/test line overlap (closed-vocabulary regime).} The file-level 5-fold splits guarantee file-level disjointness between train and test, but the coverage-repair procedure (Appendix~\ref{app:coverage-repair}) was designed to ensure that every G-code line in the test partition has been seen during training of that fold. The resulting overlap, measured at the line level (\texttt{audit/train\_test\_gcode\_overlap.json}), is high in both modes: per\_row mode has $97.6\%$ of distinct test lines and $99.0\%$ of test line-instances appearing verbatim in train (averaged across the 5 folds); full\_window mode has $97.9\%$ and $99.1\%$ at the line level. This is by design --- the 5-fold experiments evaluate the model's ability to map a sensor signal to one of the in-vocabulary G-code lines (a closed-vocabulary regime), not its ability to invent novel lines unseen during training. The genuinely open-vocabulary generalisation test is the leave-one-operation-class-out (LOCO) protocol (Section~\ref{sec:loco}); LOCO command accuracy degrades from $0.888$ on the in-distribution 5-fold to a 9-holdout mean of $0.213 \pm 0.191$ (median $0.21$), with eight of the nine holdouts in $[0.000, 0.273]$ and one outlier (\texttt{damagepocket}) at $0.678$ where the held-out class shares motion vocabulary with retained classes. LOCO numeric accuracy collapses from $0.585$ to a 9-holdout mean of $0.152$. The headline accuracy numbers should therefore be interpreted as a closed-vocabulary classification result; the LOCO numbers are the appropriate evidence for cross-operation-class generalisation.

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}
\label{sec:lim-method}

\paragraph{Frozen-encoder representation.} The encoder was trained on the same fold splits as the decoder, and its training objective (nine-class operation classification) does not include row-level G-code supervision. Both factors may limit the encoder's preservation of fine-grained per-row sensor structure. A planned encoder-retraining ablation~\cite{encoder_paper} retrains the encoder with row-level supervision and re-runs the headline 5-fold; the resulting effect size would quantify the residual encoder-side contribution and is the primary planned follow-up (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

\paragraph{Sampling rate.} Sensor data is recorded at 4~Hz. Cutting events with sub-second duration (rapid moves, small finishing passes) may be temporally aliased at this rate; for context, conventional acoustic-emission and vibration tool-condition monitoring samples at $\geq\!10$~kHz~\cite{teti2010advanced}, so the present stack is deliberately at the low-rate, low-bandwidth end of the sensing-cost spectrum and the recoverability characterisation should be read in that light. We stress that 4~Hz is \emph{not} the sole numeric-recovery bottleneck: the per-row target formulation (Section~\ref{sec:per-row}), which maps a 64-second window containing many G-code rows onto a single row's tokens, imposes a within-window row-identification ambiguity that is co-responsible for the numeric ceiling independently of sample rate (the per-digit and output-position analyses in Sections~\ref{sec:numeric-decomposition} and~\ref{sec:output-position-failure} localise both effects). A higher-rate sensor stack (deferred to the follow-up) addresses the aliasing component; the autoregressive sequence head addresses the per-row component.

\paragraph{Window length.} The 256-sample window length was inherited from the encoder paper's classification configuration~\cite{encoder_paper} to ensure consistent encoder inputs. A full window/stride sweep requires retraining the encoder for each $(\text{window}, \text{stride})$ pair and is deferred to the same encoder-retraining ablation as above.

\subsection{Statistical Power Limitations}
\label{sec:lim-power}

Per-class metrics for the command head are computed on small per-class supports in the test split: G0 ($n\!=\!86$), G1 ($n\!=\!1{,}095$), G2 ($n\!=\!475$), G3 ($n\!=\!395$), none ($n\!=\!5{,}886$). A power analysis (\texttt{audit/extended\_rigor\_audits.json}, key \texttt{rec6\_power\_analysis}) computes the minimum detectable effect (MDE) at $\alpha\!=\!0.05$, $\beta\!=\!0.20$ for each class:

\begin{itemize}
\item G0 ($n\!=\!86$, baseline $\sim$0.40): MDE $\approx 18.8$~pp. Differences below this size are not statistically distinguishable from sampling noise.
\item G1 ($n\!=\!1{,}095$, baseline $\sim$0.94): MDE $\approx 2.3$~pp.
\item G2 ($n\!=\!475$, baseline $\sim$0.50): MDE $\approx 8.0$~pp.
\item G3 ($n\!=\!395$, baseline $\sim$0.30): MDE $\approx 8.4$~pp.
\item none ($n\!=\!5{,}886$, baseline $\sim$0.95): MDE $\approx 0.9$~pp.
\item param-types X, Y, Z, R: MDE $\le 1.5$~pp (large supports).
\end{itemize}

\noindent ``Not significant'' on the long-tail classes (G0, G3) often means \emph{underpowered}, not \emph{equivalent}. The DOE-driven dataset (Appendix~\ref{app:doe}) is designed to lift per-class supports to where the per-class differences in F1 are statistically resolvable.

\subsection{Covariate-Shift Audit}
\label{sec:lim-covariate}

For each fold we compare per-field positive-support frequency between the train and test splits. The audit (\texttt{audit/extended\_rigor\_audits.json}, keys \texttt{rec7\_covariate\_shift\_*}) flags any field shifted by $\ge 3$~pp:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Per-row} mode: fold 1 shows Z at $-4.4$~pp (less Z in test than train). Folds 2--5: no flagged fields.
\item \emph{Full-window} mode: fold 1 shows F at $+3.2$~pp and fold 5 at $-5.3$~pp. Folds 2--4: no flagged fields.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The flagged shifts are modest (under 6~pp) and concentrated on the already-low-support fields (Z, F). The per-axis recoverability results in Section~\ref{sec:per-axis} should be read with this caveat in mind: small fold-to-fold variation in field coverage contributes to the cross-fold standard deviation reported in Table~\ref{tab:per_axis}.

\subsection{Per-File Performance Variance}
\label{sec:lim-per-file}

A separate concern is whether the model memorises a small set of files rather than learning the operation-level signal. We slice the 5-fold full-window test predictions by source file and report per-file accuracy (\texttt{audit/per\_file\_holdout.json}; $n=91$ files appear in at least one test split across the 5 folds). The cross-file distribution is concentrated, not heavy-tailed: command accuracy is $0.953 \pm 0.088$ across files with range $[0.571, 1.000]$ and 10th--90th percentile range $[0.846, 1.000]$; parameter-type accuracy is $0.991$ across files. Token accuracy under autoregressive decoding shows much wider per-file variance ($0.302 \pm 0.283$, range $[0.052, 0.869]$), which is the per-file signature of the same per-operation-class breakdown reported in Section~\ref{sec:per-op-class}: \texttt{face}-class files cluster at the high end and the other classes at the low end. The narrowness of the command-accuracy distribution and the absence of a heavy left tail support the interpretation that the categorical-head success is not concentrated on a small set of memorisable files: at the 10th percentile of files, command accuracy is still $0.846$.

\subsection{Evaluation Limitations}
\label{sec:lim-eval}

\paragraph{Single-run per condition.} Each operation-class file represents a single execution. The metrics in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} therefore conflate cross-condition variance (different operation classes) with cross-trial variance (rerunning the same operation). The DOE follow-up will record multiple replicates per condition, supporting variance decomposition.

\paragraph{Decoder-only inference cost.} The decoder generates token-by-token autoregressively under a grammar mask. For long sequences (full-window mode with multi-line targets) this is a substantial inference cost. A beam-search or non-autoregressive variant has not been evaluated; doing so would inform deployment-cost trade-offs (Section~\ref{sec:disc-modality}).

\subsection{Roadmap}
\label{sec:roadmap}

Planned follow-up work includes: (a)~the DOE-driven summer dataset with broader feed/depth/material coverage and higher sample rate; (b)~the encoder-retraining ablation with row-level supervision; (c)~a window/stride sweep enabled by (b); and (d)~explicit cross-platform validation on a second CNC platform now in procurement.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study characterises which components of an executing G-code instruction can be recovered from multi-modal CNC sensor data and which cannot. We introduced \modelname{}, a Transformer decoder with six structured prediction heads operating on a frozen multi-modal denoising encoder, trained on per-row supervised targets and constrained at inference time by a G-code grammar mask. Five-fold file-level cross-validated experiments on a 120-file corpus spanning nine operation classes produced four findings.

First, the \emph{categorical} structure of each instruction --- motion-command identity, axis presence, and motion direction (Section~\ref{sec:results-headline}) --- is recovered from sensors alone at $0.89$/$0.99$/$0.99$ accuracy with no positional metadata, and, because these heads are classifiers on the encoder memory rather than autoregressive predictors, the result is invariant to the teacher-forced-vs-autoregressive decoding regime. This is the deployment-robust core of the contribution.

Second, in contrast, \emph{end-to-end token and numeric} reproduction is bounded by exposure bias: it is strong under teacher-forced sweep evaluation but collapses to a corpus-modal toolpath under deployment-true autoregressive decoding (Section~\ref{sec:disc-mode-collapse}). The per-digit and probe analyses localise the bottleneck to the autoregressive sequence head, not the encoder representation, which preserves the categorical signal the robust heads exploit.

Third, the inference-time sensor-modality ablation does not differentiate modality importance for instruction-level recovery: removing any single modality at the encoder input collapses command accuracy by a uniform $40$--$44$~pp across the seven modalities tested (between-modality ANOVA $p=0.998$; every modality-vs-baseline contrast $p<0.001$), while type and parameter-type accuracy remain robust. This is a \emph{lower bound} on modality value under the current encoder; it tells us that all modalities are jointly necessary at inference but does not establish which are individually deletable in a future deployment. The per-modality encoder-retraining study required to answer the deployment question is the next planned contribution (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}); meanwhile the deployable claim is that the full multi-modal sensor stack supports the recovery story this paper reports~\cite{kimmell2025cnc,nist2023sp80082}.

Fourth, the row-level tamper detector reaches deployment-viable operating points under a simple $K=10$ majority-vote aggregator: command-swap moves from a too-noisy row-level FPR $0.22$--$0.30$ to an aggregated FPR of $0.05$--$0.15$ at near-saturated TPR under realistic alert autocorrelation (best case $0.04$ under independence). Sign-flip and feed-rate substitutions do not compound similarly. This transforms the security contribution from a marginal row-level claim into a defensible windowed-monitoring capability within the closed-vocabulary in-distribution envelope (Section~\ref{sec:disc-threat-model}).

Closing the remaining gap to full G-code reconstruction therefore requires attention at the sequence head, not at the encoder. The autoregressive collapse we observe is the mode-seeking, low-diversity degeneracy that the neural-generation literature attributes to exposure bias and likelihood-trained decoding~\cite{he2019exposure,ott2018uncertainty}; the standard remedies apply --- sequence-level training with policy-gradient updates (MIXER~\cite{ranzato2016sequence}), nucleus / top-$p$ sampling~\cite{holtzman2020curious}, unlikelihood training~\cite{welleck2020neural}, scheduled sampling at higher noise ratios, or a non-autoregressive prediction stream --- combined with (a) data with richer continuous-parameter variation, (b) higher sensor sample rate, and (c) row-level encoder pre-training. A designed-experiment data-collection campaign (Appendix~\ref{app:doe}) addresses (a) and (b) by re-instrumenting the testbed with both denser variation and a higher sampling-rate sensor stack. The encoder-retraining ablation flagged in Section~\ref{sec:future} addresses (c) and is the next planned contribution.

\section{Coverage-Repair Split Procedure}
\label{app:coverage-repair}

File-level stratified $k$-fold splits prevent within-file leakage but do not guarantee \emph{token coverage}: a held-out fold may contain G-code lines that never appear in the corresponding training partner, in which case the decoder is being evaluated on a vocabulary it has not had the opportunity to learn. The coverage-repair procedure removes this confound while preserving the file-level disjointness.

Given a candidate (train, test) partition for fold $i$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), leftmargin=*]
\item Tokenise the G-code text of every window in both splits and compute $U_{\text{test}}$ and $U_{\text{train}}$, the sets of distinct G-code lines occurring in each.
\item Let $M = U_{\text{test}} \setminus U_{\text{train}}$ be the set of test-only lines.
\item If $M = \emptyset$, accept the partition. Otherwise identify the file $f^\star \in \text{test}$ contributing the largest number of $M$-lines, and the file $g^\star \in \text{train}$ whose removal-and-replacement minimally perturbs the per-class fold proportions. Swap $f^\star$ and $g^\star$.
\item Recompute $M$ and repeat until $M = \emptyset$ or until a swap-budget of $10$ is exhausted (in which case the residual $M$-lines are recorded as a "novel-vocabulary at test" diagnostic but the partition is accepted).
\end{enumerate}

In practice the procedure converges in 0--3 swaps per fold on the present corpus. Recorded fold sizes after coverage repair differ from the stratified target by at most one file per class.
