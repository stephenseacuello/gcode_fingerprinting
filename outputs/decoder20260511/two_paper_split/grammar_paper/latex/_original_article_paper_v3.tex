\begin{abstract}
G-code controls nearly all CNC manufacturing, yet standard tokenizers fail on it: 97\% of tokens are numeric coordinates, vocabulary scales with part geometry, and subword splitting destroys semantic units. These failures reflect a broader \emph{Technical Language Processing} (TLP) challenge in which NLP tools trained on natural-language corpora break down on formally specified technical languages.

We make three contributions. First, we formalize G-code toolpath syntax as a regular grammar and propose a three-level tokenization framework: a finite-state grammar enforcing type transitions and command-parameter associations, a hierarchical decomposition into type, identity, and value, and digit-by-digit numeric prediction that reduces the structural vocabulary to 19 tokens regardless of geometry. Second, we present a \emph{design-space analysis} organized around three properties any practical G-code tokenizer should provide---bounded vocabulary, lossless coordinate representation, and value-level grammar validity---and show by case analysis over the three dominant tokenizer classes (atomic, subword, digit-decomposed) that only structured digit decomposition occupies the corner where all three hold simultaneously. The framework is therefore a principled choice among the standard families, not one design among many.

Third, we verify every cell of the design-space table empirically. Across seven tokenizer families on 120 CNC milling files, only digit-decomposed and character-level tokenizers achieve zero held-out OOV; BPE families reach 0.25--4.81\% and flat per-value collapses to 18--65\%. Across five cross-validation folds and more than 660 test samples, decoders trained with the standard HuggingFace BPE pipeline (whitespace pre-tokenizer) reach $0.00 \pm 0.00\%$ free-generation grammar validity due to decoder-side whitespace reinsertion between subword pieces, while a byte-level BPE control that replaces the pre-tokenizer recovers to $97.1\%$---indistinguishable from the other non-fragmenting rows ($96.98$--$98.31\%$). This separates the standard-pipeline failure (a decode-time artifact, fixable) from the merge-table-ambiguity concern (not observed empirically on this corpus). The KL divergence between grammar-masked and unmasked output distributions is $0.005 \pm 0.003$ nats, two orders of magnitude below the regime where ASAp-style corrections become necessary.

Under 5-fold cross-validation on the full curriculum-trained pipeline, the hierarchical decoder reaches 94.7\% token accuracy and 86.5\% sequence exact-match, a 19.8-point improvement over a flat 712-class baseline on the same architecture. Structural predictions exceed 96\% and numeric digit prediction reaches 91.1\%. A slim cross-entropy trainer used for the apples-to-apples cross-tokenizer experiments (Section~\ref{sec:exp_c}) reaches a lower absolute accuracy; we report both regimes separately and do not conflate them.
\end{abstract}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} G-code, CNC machining, tokenization, language model, grammar constraints, hierarchical decoding, manufacturing

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Numerical control of machine tools dates to the early 1950s, when the Parsons Corporation and MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory developed the first NC milling machine~\cite{parsons_nc}. The programming language that emerged---G-code, standardized as RS-274~\cite{rs274d} and later ISO~6983~\cite{iso6983}---has since become the standard instruction set for CNC milling, turning, and multi-axis machining. Large language models now process Python, Java, SQL, and dozens of other languages~\cite{codex,codellama,starcoder}, yet G-code remains virtually untouched by the language modeling community.

The reason is structural: in a typical CNC milling vocabulary, 693 of 712 tokens (97.3\%) are numeric coordinates; only 19 cover commands, parameters, and control symbols. BPE~\cite{bpe} and WordPiece~\cite{wordpiece} split \texttt{X3.275} into \texttt{[X, 3, ., 275]}---destroying the semantic unit (Table~\ref{tab:bpe_failure}; confirmed empirically in Table~\ref{tab:tokenizer_comparison}). Worse, vocabulary scales with geometry: every new workpiece introduces unseen coordinates and out-of-vocabulary failures. This is an instance of the broader \emph{Technical Language Processing} (TLP) challenge~\cite{tlp_foundational}: standard NLP tools, trained on natural-language corpora, fail systematically on domain-specific technical languages whose vocabulary, syntax, and semantics diverge from general text.

\paragraph{Why tokenization of G-code matters now.}
Fixing tokenization is a prerequisite for four downstream applications that have recently become active research targets in CNC monitoring, each of which breaks in a specific way when numeric coordinates are fragmented. (i)~\emph{Process verification} in regulated industries (aerospace, medical devices, defense) requires demonstrating that the intended toolpath, feed rate, and depth of cut actually occurred on the machine; classification-based monitoring~\cite{cnc_anomaly,tool_wear,vibration} can confirm \emph{what operation type} ran but not \emph{which specific G-code line} was executed, and the upgrade from operation-level to line-level verification requires a decoder that can reconstruct G-code tokens from sensor windows---a task that fails immediately if the token stream cannot round-trip to a parseable line. (ii)~\emph{Manufacturing cybersecurity} detects subtle G-code tampering (coordinate perturbations, command substitutions, feed-rate modifications) that evade sensor-only anomaly detectors precisely because the machine executes the modified program faithfully and sensors report normal dynamics; comparing a claimed program against a sensor-reconstructed one creates an independent physical verification channel, which again demands grammatically valid reconstruction at the value level. (iii)~\emph{Cost-effective distributed retrofit monitoring} needs sensor selection criteria that go beyond operation classification to measure each modality's contribution to program-level reconstruction, which is only well-posed when the prediction target is a structured G-code sequence rather than a class label. (iv)~\emph{Digital twins and toolpath fingerprinting} treat the forward map (G-code $\to$ sensor stream) and its inverse (sensor stream $\to$ G-code) as dual views of the same physical process; an ill-formed inverse output---whitespace-corrupted subwords that no parser will accept---breaks the digital-twin closure property. All four applications share a common requirement: translation between symbolic program semantics (commands, addresses, numeric values) and continuous physical measurements must preserve syntactic validity at the parser level. That is the problem this paper addresses.

Our framework rests on three observations:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{The language is simple.} Despite hundreds of defined G-codes, real toolpaths use four motion commands (G0, G1, G2, G3). The per-line grammar is regular and capturable by a finite-state automaton (Sections~\ref{sec:anatomy}--\ref{sec:grammar}).

    \item \textbf{Structure and values are separable.} Selecting which command and which parameters is a small discrete problem; predicting coordinate values to micrometer precision is continuous. Each benefits from a different mechanism (Sections~\ref{sec:tokenization}--\ref{sec:decomposition}).

    \item \textbf{Numbers are structured objects.} A coordinate is a composition of sign and digits, not an atomic symbol. Digit-by-digit prediction eliminates vocabulary explosion and generalizes to unseen geometry (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}).
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}) provides an end-to-end overview. Specifically, we contribute:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item A formal regular grammar for G-code toolpath lines, with five deterministic syntactic rules (Section~\ref{sec:grammar}).
    \item A design-space characterization of G-code tokenizers: three properties (bounded vocabulary, lossless coordinates, grammar validity) and a case analysis over the three dominant tokenizer classes showing that only digit-level decomposition occupies the corner where all three properties hold simultaneously (Section~\ref{sec:design_space}). The remainder of the paper provides constructive and empirical support for that corner.
    \item A hierarchical tokenization decomposing tokens into type, identity, and digit-level value---reducing structural vocabulary to 19 fixed tokens versus 712+ that scale with geometry (Sections~\ref{sec:tokenization}--\ref{sec:decomposition}).
    \item A grammar-constrained decoding mechanism combining hard logit masks and soft training penalties, guaranteeing syntactic validity at $O(1)$ cost per step for the covered grammar fragment (Section~\ref{sec:constrained_decoding}).
    \item Empirical validation on 120 G-code files from Autodesk Fusion~360 CAM running on a TinyG-controlled Bantam Tools Desktop CNC mill: a tokenizer-zoo evaluation populating every cell of the design-space table, plus 2.5$\times$ token reduction over GPT-4's BPE, 99.9\% grammar coverage on the evaluation corpus, Heaps' law vocabulary scaling~\cite{heaps1978}, and downstream decoder performance of 94.7\% token accuracy with structural predictions exceeding 96\% (Section~\ref{sec:empirical}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.}
The grammar, precision configuration, and empirical evaluation in this paper cover a specific subset of RS-274D G-code: the four core motion commands (\texttt{G0}--\texttt{G3}) with five positional and feed-rate parameter addresses (\texttt{X}, \texttt{Y}, \texttt{Z}, \texttt{F}, \texttt{R}), plus five auxiliary literals for program setup and shutdown. Our 120-file dataset comes from one CAM system (Fusion~360), one firmware family (TinyG; we have also used grbl on the same hardware), and one machine class (a desktop-scale 3-axis mill with a $\sim$140$\times$102$\times$60\,mm work envelope). Canned cycles (\texttt{G81}--\texttt{G89}), tool compensation (\texttt{G41}/\texttt{G42}), \texttt{I}/\texttt{J}/\texttt{K} arc-center format, full 5-axis programs, and controller-specific dialects (Fanuc, Siemens, Mazak, Haas) are outside the current grammar but extend naturally via additional lexical categories and DFA states (discussed in Sections~\ref{sec:grammar} and~\ref{sec:discussion}). Claims about vocabulary growth, grammar coverage, and held-out OOV should be read in this scope; we discuss generalization to larger work envelopes and alternative precision configurations in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Technical Language Processing.}
Brundage et al.~\cite{tlp_foundational} define TLP as the adaptation of NLP tools to technical domains where standard methods fail due to specialized vocabulary, non-standard syntax, and semantic divergence from general text. The core TLP research program addresses \emph{human-written} technical prose. Hodkiewicz and Ho~\cite{tlp_cleaning} show that maintenance work orders suffer from misspellings, inconsistent abbreviations, and missing fields; Sexton et al.~\cite{tlp_keyword} benchmark keyword extraction methods on these records; Sharp et al.~\cite{tlp_semiautonomous} develop semi-autonomous tagging for unstructured maintenance logs. Subsequent work extends TLP to equipment reliability~\cite{tlp_nuclear}, fault diagnosis~\cite{tlp_fault}, and broader technical text adaptation~\cite{tlp_adapting}. In all these cases, the NLP failure mode is \emph{lexical}---jargon, abbreviations, and domain-specific terminology that standard tokenizers and taggers do not recognize.

G-code presents a fundamentally different TLP challenge. It is not human-written prose but a \emph{formally specified machine language} with deterministic syntax (Section~\ref{sec:grammar}). The failure mode is not lexical but \emph{structural}: 97\% of tokens are numeric coordinates whose vocabulary scales with geometry, and standard subword tokenizers destroy semantic units rather than misrecognizing them (Table~\ref{tab:bpe_failure}). Custom dictionaries and entity taggers---the standard TLP toolkit---cannot address this. Our work extends TLP to formal technical languages, where adaptation requires formal grammars and structured numeric prediction rather than domain lexicons.

\textbf{LLMs for G-code and CNC.}
Jignasu et al.~\cite{gcode_foundational} evaluate six LLMs on G-code comprehension, debugging, and manipulation for additive manufacturing, finding wide performance variation---GPT-4 identifies errors readily, while smaller models fail even with additional prompting.
GLLM~\cite{gllm} fine-tunes StarCoder-3B~\cite{starcoder} for natural-language-to-G-code translation but retains BPE, which fragments coordinates destructively (Table~\ref{tab:bpe_failure}). Image2Gcode~\cite{image2gcode} generates toolpaths via diffusion, bypassing tokenization entirely. ChatCNC~\cite{chatcnc} uses LLMs for CNC monitoring but consumes rather than generates G-code. Broader ML work in CNC targets anomaly detection~\cite{cnc_anomaly}, tool wear~\cite{tool_wear}, and vibration analysis~\cite{vibration}---all consuming sensor data without modeling G-code as a language. None address tokenization; our framework is complementary to all of them.

\textbf{Numeric tokenization.}
Subword methods fragment numbers destructively~\cite{nogueira_numbers}. Singh and Strouse~\cite{tokenization_counts} show that tokenization strategy---particularly digit-level granularity and right-to-left ordering---substantially impacts arithmetic performance in frontier LLMs. Alternatives include xVal~\cite{xval} (scalar multiplier on a \texttt{<NUM>} token), per-digit decomposition for mathematical reasoning~\cite{digit_token}, single-token embeddings~\cite{single_token_numbers}, and regression losses~\cite{regress_dont_guess}. Cognetta and Okazaki~\cite{tokenization_fst} formalize BPE and WordPiece as finite-state transducers, connecting subword tokenization to regular-language theory. We combine digit-level decomposition with domain-specific conditioning (parameter type, operation context) and grammar constraints. Unlike xVal, our digit heads attach to any encoder; unlike regression, categorical digits naturally express per-position uncertainty.

\textbf{Design-space analyses in related areas.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} is structurally similar in spirit---though not in formal rigor---to well-known design-space results in distributed systems and formal language theory. The CAP theorem~\cite{cap_brewer,cap_gilbert_lynch} organizes distributed storage around three properties (consistency, availability, partition tolerance) and identifies named operating corners that real systems must choose between. Chomsky's hierarchy~\cite{chomsky_hierarchy} organizes formal languages by containments that mirror the expressive power of their recognizers. We invoke these precedents only as framing: our trichotomy is a constructive case analysis over the three dominant tokenizer classes (atomic, subword, digit-decomposed), not a theorem over a formally defined tokenizer model, and we do not claim impossibility in the CAP or FLP sense. What our analysis does establish is that each of the three standard classes deterministically fails at least one of the three properties in Definitions~\ref{def:bounded}--\ref{def:grammar_valid}, whereas digit decomposition satisfies all three by construction. The empirical battery in Section~\ref{sec:empirical} populates every cell of the resulting table and confirms the predicted ranking.

\textbf{Grammar-constrained decoding.}
PICARD~\cite{picard} constrains SQL to valid parse trees; Outlines~\cite{outlines} compiles regular expressions into FSMs; XGrammar~\cite{xgrammar} handles context-free grammars via pushdown automata. Park et al.~\cite{grammar_aligned} show na\"ive masking distorts distributions and propose ASAp; their follow-up~\cite{flexible_gcd} achieves 17.7$\times$ faster preprocessing. Geng et al.~\cite{gcd_input_dependent} extend the paradigm with input-dependent grammars; SynCode~\cite{syncode} precomputes DFA-based mask stores for efficient enforcement. G-code is \emph{regular}, not context-free, so our constraints require no compilation and evaluate in $O(1)$. Moreover, they are \emph{semantic}---``G0 forbids F'', ``G2 requires R''---not purely syntactic. Because most transitions are near-deterministic (\textsc{Parameter} $\to$ \textsc{Numeric} is mandatory), we expected the distribution distortion Park et al.\ identify to be small. We confirm this empirically in Section~\ref{sec:kl_measurement}: across 5 folds the mean KL divergence between the grammar-masked and unmasked type distributions is $0.005 \pm 0.003$ nats, and the unmasked model places only $0.14\%$ of its probability mass on grammar-disallowed types---an order of magnitude below the regimes where ASAp-style corrections are necessary.

\section{Background and Formal Grammar}
\label{sec:anatomy}

\subsection{G-Code program structure}
\label{sec:program_structure}

We ground our design in the structure of real programs. Our dataset comprises 120 G-code files from Autodesk Fusion~360 CAM for a Bantam Tools Desktop CNC mill, covering face milling, pocket milling, and adaptive clearing at two feed rate settings.\footnote{Dataset and implementation available at \url{https://github.com/stephenseacuello/gcode\_fingerprinting}.} The 120 files contain 62{,}470 toolpath lines, but only 2{,}248 of those lines are unique after canonicalization---a ratio of $\sim$28-to-1. This duplication is not an artifact of our preprocessing; it is a structural feature of CAM-generated G-code, and it has direct implications for tokenizer evaluation.

CAM postprocessors generate G-code by sweeping a tool along a parameterized geometric path---a contour, a pocket boundary, an adaptive clearing pattern. The same coordinate values appear repeatedly because the tool returns to the same fixture features (entry retracts to the same safe Z, periodic clearance moves to the same staging point, repeat passes at incremented depths). Adaptive clearing in particular emits long sequences of short linear cuts whose start and end coordinates differ from one another by less than the discriminative resolution of the tokenizer's quantization, so many lines collapse to identical canonical forms after rounding to the supported precision. Modal persistence (Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}) compresses the duplicates further: many lines omit the explicit motion command and rely on the previous \texttt{G1}, so two lines that differ only in their leading command word fold to the same parameter list.

This 28-to-1 compression matters for tokenizer evaluation in two ways. First, it means a tokenizer's vocabulary growth (Experiment~A) is dominated by the unique lines, not by the raw line count: a flat-per-value tokenizer that emits 4{,}435 distinct tokens on the 120 files would emit nominally up to 62{,}470 if every line were unique, so the realized growth is roughly $1/14$ of the worst case. Second, it means that two lines that look identical after canonicalization are not necessarily identical at the sensor-aligned level: the same canonical \texttt{X1.4919 Y1.4848} can appear at many different points in the toolpath, paired with different sensor-window prefixes, and the cross-validation splits respect program boundaries rather than canonical-line boundaries to prevent trivial within-program memorization. We return to this point in Appendix~\ref{sec:reproducibility}.

A typical program has three phases (Listing~\ref{lst:program_anatomy}).

\subsubsection{Preamble: Setup Codes}

The preamble sets coordinate mode (G90/G91), units (G20/G21), plane (G17--G19), work coordinate system (G54--G59), tool, and spindle. These codes appear once and constitute 5--15 lines---a small, fixed vocabulary requiring no numeric decomposition.

The toolpath---the cutting portion of every CNC program---is built from remarkably few commands:

\subsubsection{Modal Persistence}

Motion commands are \emph{modal}: once issued, they persist until overridden. In Listing~\ref{lst:program_anatomy}, \texttt{X3.291 Z0.0003} carries no explicit command---it executes under the previously active \texttt{G1}. In our data, approximately 60\% of toolpath lines omit the command word. The grammar must therefore allow sequences beginning directly with a \textsc{Parameter} token (Figure~\ref{fig:modal_state}).

\subsubsection{Auxiliary Commands, Postamble, and Summary}

\emph{Canned cycles} (G81--G89) provide shorthand for drilling and boring; the controller firmware expands each into a deterministic G0/G1 sequence.\footnote{Tapping cycles (G84) additionally require spindle synchronization---a firmware operation outside the G0--G3 grammar.} The \emph{postamble} returns the machine to a safe state: spindle off (M5), coolant off (M9), home (G53~G0), program end (M30). Our tokenizer handles these as literal tokens without numeric decomposition. In our dataset, the toolpath dominates program content (80--90\% of lines), with preamble (5--10\%) and postamble (2--5\%) as fixed overhead---justifying our focus on the four core motion commands rather than the full RS-274D standard.

\subsection{Formal grammar}
\label{sec:grammar}

Given the structural regularity established in Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}, we now formalize toolpath lines as lexical categories, syntactic rules, and a production grammar.

\subsubsection{Lexical Categories}

Four token types partition the vocabulary:

We separate addresses from values as the key design choice. \texttt{X3.275} becomes two tokens: \texttt{X} (\textsc{Parameter}) encoding \emph{which axis}, and \texttt{NUM\_X\_3275} (\textsc{Numeric}) encoding \emph{how far}---fundamentally different information that benefits from independent prediction.

\subsubsection{Syntactic Rules}

Toolpath syntax follows five deterministic rules:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Type Transitions.} \textsc{Command}~$\to$~\textsc{Parameter}/\texttt{EOS}; \textsc{Parameter}~$\to$~\textsc{Numeric}; \textsc{Numeric}~$\to$~\textsc{Parameter}/\texttt{EOS}. Commands and numerics never appear adjacent.

    \item \textbf{Modal Group Exclusion.} At most one command from each modal group may appear per block. The motion group \{\texttt{G0}, \texttt{G1}, \texttt{G2}, \texttt{G3}\} is mutually exclusive.

    \item \textbf{Command-Parameter Association.} Each command defines which parameters are valid, required, or forbidden (Table~\ref{tab:core_commands}).

    \item \textbf{Single-Letter Rule.} Each parameter address appears at most once per block. A line cannot contain two \texttt{X} values.

    \item \textbf{Modal Persistence.} If no command is specified, the previously active modal command persists. A line containing only \texttt{X3.291 Z0.0003} executes under the last issued motion command.
\end{enumerate}

RS-274D defines several modal groups (motion: G0--G3; plane: G17--G19; distance: G90/G91; units: G20/G21; coordinate system: G54--G59); commands within a group are mutually exclusive. Only the motion group matters during toolpath generation; the rest are set once in the preamble. Rules~1--4 apply within a single block. Rule~5 introduces cross-line state: the active modal command carries over from previous blocks. We treat each line independently, passing the modal command as external context.

\subsubsection{Command-Parameter Associations and Execution Order}

Parameter associations per the RS-274D standard~\cite{rs274d} and the NIST interpreter specification~\cite{nist_ngc} are formalized in Table~\ref{tab:core_commands}. Our framework implements X, Y, Z, F, and R; additional addresses (I, J, K, S, P) can be added per controller dialect with appropriate precision settings (Table~\ref{tab:precision}). Forbidden entries ($\times$) become $-\infty$ logit masks; required entries ($\bullet$) become positive biases (Section~\ref{sec:constrained_decoding}). The standard specifies a 21-step execution order within each block~\cite{nist_ngc}, with motion (G0--G3) at step~20 and stop codes last; our grammar does not model intra-block ordering.

\subsubsection{Formal Grammar Definition}
\label{sec:formal_grammar}

We define the per-line G-code grammar as a right-linear grammar $\mathcal{G} = (\Sigma, V, S, P)$~\cite{sipser}:

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Terminal alphabet.}
$\Sigma = \{\texttt{BOS}, \texttt{EOS}, \texttt{G0}, \texttt{G1}, \texttt{G2}, \texttt{G3}, \texttt{X}, \texttt{Y}, \texttt{Z}, \texttt{F}, \texttt{R}, \texttt{NUM}\}$

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Nonterminals.}
$V = \{S, \mathit{Body}, \mathit{Cmd}, \mathit{Params}, \mathit{Param}, \mathit{Addr}\}$

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Start symbol.} $S$

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Productions.}
\begin{align*}
S &\to \texttt{BOS}\; \mathit{Body}\; \texttt{EOS} \\
\mathit{Body} &\to \mathit{Cmd}\; \mathit{Params} \mid \mathit{Params} \\
\mathit{Cmd} &\to \texttt{G0} \mid \texttt{G1} \mid \texttt{G2} \mid \texttt{G3} \\
\mathit{Params} &\to \mathit{Addr}\; \texttt{NUM}\; \mathit{Params} \mid \varepsilon \\
\mathit{Addr} &\to \texttt{X} \mid \texttt{Y} \mid \texttt{Z} \mid \texttt{F} \mid \texttt{R}
\end{align*}

\noindent Every production is right-linear (at most one nonterminal, rightmost), so $\mathcal{G}$ generates a regular language~\cite{sipser}. The $\mathit{Addr}$ production shows the five currently implemented addresses; additional addresses (I, J, K, S, P) extend $\Sigma$ without changing the grammar's regular structure. The grammar is recognized by a five-state deterministic finite automaton whose state table, transition function, soundness and completeness arguments, and cross-line modal extension we defer to Appendix~\ref{sec:dfa_construction}; what matters here is that the constraint mask $M$ used by Section~\ref{sec:constrained_decoding} implements $\delta$ directly, with $M(q_i, t) = -\infty$ whenever $\delta(q_i, t)$ is undefined, and that mask lookup is $O(1)$ since $|Q| = 5$ and $|\Sigma| = 12$.

The grammar defines 19 structural terminals: 5 special (\texttt{PAD}, \texttt{BOS}, \texttt{EOS}, \texttt{UNK}, \texttt{MASK}), 4 commands (\texttt{G0}--\texttt{G3}), 5 parameters (\texttt{X}, \texttt{Y}, \texttt{Z}, \texttt{F}, \texttt{R}), and 5 auxiliary literals (\texttt{G53}, \texttt{M3}, \texttt{M5}, \texttt{M6}, \texttt{M30}). Of these, 7--9 appear in any given dataset (Table~\ref{tab:vocab_growth}); the remainder are reserved for future extensions. Numeric values are predicted by digit heads (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}), not vocabulary entries. As a worked example, Figure~\ref{fig:grammar_constraints} traces generation of \texttt{G2 X3.5 Y0.7 R0.08}: after \textsc{Parameter}~\texttt{X}, only \textsc{Numeric} is valid; after the numeric token, \texttt{X} is masked (already used) and \texttt{R} is boosted (required for G2). Each constraint evaluates in $O(1)$.

\section{A Design-Space Analysis of G-Code Tokenizers}
\label{sec:design_space}

Sections~\ref{sec:anatomy}--\ref{sec:grammar} establish what G-code is. We now analyze the space of possible tokenizers around three properties any practical G-code tokenizer should provide, and show by case analysis over the three dominant tokenizer classes (atomic, subword, digit-decomposed) that only the third class satisfies all three properties simultaneously. We do not claim an impossibility theorem in the formal sense of CAP or FLP: the case analysis is over the classes that dominate current practice, not over a formally defined space of all conceivable tokenizers, and the argument is constructive in that it names what each class gives up. Section~\ref{sec:empirical} verifies each cell of Table~\ref{tab:design_space} empirically.

\subsection{Three properties}
\label{sec:three_properties}

We define three properties any practical G-code tokenizer should provide. Each definition isolates a single failure mode of standard tokenization and is given a precise, testable form so that Section~\ref{sec:empirical} can populate it with a real measurement.

\begin{definition}[Bounded vocabulary]
\label{def:bounded}
A tokenizer $T$ has \emph{bounded vocabulary} if there exists a constant $K$, independent of geometry, such that for every G-code corpus $\mathcal{C}$ at fixed precision $p$,
\[
    |V_T(\mathcal{C})| \;\le\; K,
\]
where $V_T(\mathcal{C})$ denotes the set of distinct token types $T$ emits on $\mathcal{C}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Lossless coordinates]
\label{def:lossless}
A tokenizer $T$ is \emph{lossless on coordinates} if for every numeric value $v$ at supported precision $p$, the encode--decode roundtrip is exact:
\[
    \mathrm{dec}_T(\mathrm{enc}_T(v)) \;=\; v,
\]
equivalently $\sup_{v}\, |\mathrm{dec}_T(\mathrm{enc}_T(v)) - v| = 0$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Grammar validity]
\label{def:grammar_valid}
A tokenizer $T$ \emph{supports grammar validity} if both of the following hold:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=(\roman*)]
    \item every line $\ell \in L(\mathcal{G})$ admits an unambiguous encoding $\mathrm{enc}_T(\ell) \in V_T^{\ast}$;
    \item the per-step output distribution under $T$ can be restricted---via logit masking or an equivalent constraint mechanism---to the set $L(\mathcal{G})$ without removing any line in $L(\mathcal{G})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Property~(ii) is non-trivial. A tokenizer that maps every coordinate to a single $\langle\mathrm{NUM}\rangle$ token (e.g.\ xVal~\cite{xval}) admits encodings, but the value-level distribution cannot be grammar-constrained: the constraint mechanism has no token at which to apply the mask. Subword tokenizers fail property (i): the same numeric value may decompose differently in different contexts, so the encoding is not unambiguous at the value level.

\subsection{A structural characterization}
\label{sec:impossibility}

The three properties of Section~\ref{sec:three_properties} look modest in isolation. We now show by case analysis over the three dominant tokenizer classes that only one class admits all three properties simultaneously.

\begin{proposition}[Tokenizer trichotomy over dominant classes]
\label{prop:trichotomy}
Let $T$ be a tokenizer for G-code coordinates at fixed precision $p$ over an unbounded geometric domain, drawn from one of the three standard classes: atomic (one token per coordinate value), subword (BPE- or WordPiece-style merges over the coordinate string), or digit-decomposed (each value emitted as an address letter followed by a fixed-length digit sequence). Among these three classes, only digit-decomposed tokenizers with a finite sub-vocabulary closed under composition (e.g., decimal digits with sign) satisfy Definitions~\ref{def:bounded}, \ref{def:lossless}, and~\ref{def:grammar_valid} simultaneously.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Case analysis]
We argue by case over the three named classes of $T$. For each failing class we name the definition it violates and the structural reason.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Case 1: atomic tokenization.} Suppose $T$ treats each coordinate as a single token. The set of quantized coordinate values at precision $p$ over an unbounded domain is countably infinite, so $T$ must either collapse infinitely many values onto finitely many tokens (violating Definition~\ref{def:lossless}) or preserve every value atomically and let $|V_T|$ scale with the observed geometric range (violating Definition~\ref{def:bounded}). Either branch fails.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Case 2: subword tokenization.} BPE and WordPiece fragment numeric strings into merge-derived subword pieces. The fragmentation has two distinct failure modes that we separate empirically in Section~\ref{sec:exp_c}. \emph{Sub-case~2a (decode-side reinsertion):} standard HuggingFace BPE pipelines use a whitespace pre-tokenizer whose inverse inserts whitespace between pieces on decode, so an in-vocabulary value like $\texttt{0.1755}$ decodes to \texttt{"0 . 1755"} and is no longer parseable as a G-code word. This fails Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} property~(i) immediately, and empirically reaches $0.00 \pm 0.00\%$ free-generation grammar validity (Table~\ref{tab:exp_c}). \emph{Sub-case~2b (value-level constraint boundary):} even with a byte-level decoder that preserves the contiguous numeric string, the merge table is data-driven and context-dependent; identical values may decompose differently across contexts, and there is no consistent token slot that corresponds to a single coordinate value. This leaves Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} property~(ii) unsatisfied at the value level: the constraint mechanism has no semantically meaningful slot at which to enforce per-value rules (e.g., ``if this is an arc parameter, mask values outside $[0, R_{\max}]$''). Subword tokenization can be made to pass property~(i) by switching the decoder configuration---as the byte-level BPE row of Table~\ref{tab:exp_c} demonstrates---but it does not pass Definition~\ref{def:bounded} (active vocabulary grows with geometry) or the value-level form of property~(ii) simultaneously.

\smallskip\noindent\textit{Case 3: structured decomposition.} Among the three classes, only digit decomposition encodes each value as a finite alphabet that composes back to any value at precision $p$. The smallest such alphabet is the set of decimal digits together with a sign symbol, an 11-element sub-vocabulary closed under composition. Definition~\ref{def:bounded} holds because the alphabet is constant in $|\mathcal{C}|$. Definition~\ref{def:lossless} holds because digit composition is exact at precision $p$. Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} holds because each digit position is itself a discrete prediction slot that admits per-position masking.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Scope of the case analysis.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} covers the three classes that dominate current NLP practice (atomic, subword, digit-decomposed) and character-level tokenization as a degenerate sub-case of decomposition over the ASCII alphabet (evaluated empirically in Section~\ref{sec:empirical}). It does not cover every conceivable tokenizer design: learned discrete codebooks, hash-based tokenization, and continuous scalar heads~\cite{xval} each require a separate argument, which we address briefly in the remarks below. The claim is therefore a \emph{characterization over the standard families}, not a universal impossibility result; the constructive contribution is naming what each class gives up rather than proving any class cannot exist.

\begin{proposition}[Fused-subword corollary]
\label{prop:fused_subword}
Let $T$ be a subword tokenizer whose learned merge table produces at least one token whose surface form fuses an address letter with one or more numeric digits (e.g., a single token spanning \texttt{X3}, \texttt{Y0}, or \texttt{R0}). Then $T$ does not admit a consistent token-position slot at which a per-value constraint mask can be applied, and therefore fails Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} property~(ii) at the value level, regardless of whether its decoder preserves numeric contiguity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A per-value constraint mask (e.g., ``if this is an arc radius, mask values outside $[R_{\min}, R_{\max}]$'') must be evaluated at a position where the decoder is choosing a coordinate value, conditioned on already having committed to an address. Fused address--digit tokens collapse the address commitment and (part of) the value into a single token choice, so the mask would have to condition on the identity of the token currently being chosen rather than on a previously emitted address---which is not what value-level constraints do in any standard grammar-constrained decoding framework~\cite{outlines,xgrammar,picard}. A consistent slot would require either (a) refusing to learn any fused address--digit merge, which is not a property enforced by BPE or WordPiece training, or (b) post-hoc splitting the merge at inference, which requires tokenizer-specific logic and breaks the $O(1)$ mask-lookup property. The byte-level BPE row of Table~\ref{tab:exp_c} empirically produces fused merges of this form (Table~\ref{tab:bpe_failure} shows \texttt{X3}, \texttt{Y0}, \texttt{R0}), so even though it passes property~(i), it fails property~(ii) at the value level.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:fused_subword} formalizes what the byte-level BPE result empirically shows: the grammar-validity gap between BPE configurations is a property-(i) issue solved by the decoder configuration, but the grammar-\emph{constrainability} gap between BPE and digit decomposition is a property-(ii) issue that survives any decoder configuration, because the underlying merge table has already collapsed the address--value boundary.

\paragraph{Remarks on the case analysis.}
The characterization is robust to several variations. \textit{Variable per-parameter precision} (Table~\ref{tab:precision}) does not change the argument: each address $a$ gets its own digit-position count $n_a$, the operative alphabet is still the eleven decimal digits plus sign, and the decomposed sub-vocabulary remains constant in $|\mathcal{C}|$. \textit{Mixed atomic-and-decomposed hybrids} inherit the worst failure of each component: any atomically encoded address still has unbounded geometric range and fails Definition~\ref{def:bounded}. \textit{Structured tokenizers with reserved literals} (such as ours, which adds 19 fixed literals to an 11-element digit alphabet) preserve Definition~\ref{def:bounded} because the literals are constant in $|\mathcal{C}|$.

Two structural classes lie outside the three named classes of Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} and warrant separate comment. \emph{Continuous-valued tokenizers} such as xVal~\cite{xval} replace the value-level token with a scalar head; Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} property~(ii) fails by inspection because the constraint mechanism has no discrete token slot at which to apply a value-level mask. \emph{Learned discrete codebooks} (e.g., VQ-style value quantizers) can in principle occupy the all-checkmarks corner if the codebook is closed under composition, but we do not evaluate them in this work. The unquantized limit $p \to 0$ has an uncountable value space, so Definition~\ref{def:bounded} fails for every token-based tokenizer; our reliance on a fixed positive $p$ is a necessary precondition for the all-checkmarks corner to exist at all.

Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} characterizes the framework's design choices as the natural corner for current tokenizer practice: among the three classes standard NLP systems actually use, digit decomposition is the one that occupies the (\textsc{Bounded}, \textsc{Lossless}, \textsc{Grammar}) corner simultaneously, and the grammar-constrained decoding of Section~\ref{sec:constrained_decoding} is what activates property~(ii) of Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid}.

Section~\ref{sec:exp_c} documents both sub-failures of Case~2 empirically. Standard whitespace-pre-tokenized BPE reaches $0.00 \pm 0.00\%$ free-generation grammar validity across 5 folds and 660+ test samples because of decoder whitespace reinsertion (sub-case~2a); a byte-level BPE control that avoids that artifact is included to isolate the merge-table ambiguity (sub-case~2b) from the decoder configuration.

Domain BPE tokenizes \texttt{R0.1755} into the five subword pieces \texttt{[R, 0, ., 17, 55]}, all of which are perfectly retrievable, and a trained decoder under this tokenizer routinely predicts the right subword sequence in our experiments. \section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the instantiation of the digit-decomposition corner of Table~\ref{tab:design_space}: the canonicalization and hybrid tokenization step (Section~\ref{sec:tokenization}), the hierarchical token decomposition that occupies Case~3 of Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}), and the grammar-constrained decoding mechanism that activates property~(ii) of Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} (Section~\ref{sec:constrained_decoding}).

\subsection{Tokenization scheme}
\label{sec:tokenization}

\subsubsection{Canonicalization}

Raw G-code undergoes five normalization steps to eliminate surface variation across CAM systems: (1)~strip comments (\texttt{;} and \texttt{(...)}), (2)~strip line numbers (\texttt{N}-codes) and checksums, (3)~uppercase, (4)~normalize leading zeros on command codes (\texttt{G01}~$\to$~\texttt{G1}), and (5)~collapse whitespace.

\noindent For example, \texttt{n20 g02 x3.275 r0.083 ; arc} canonicalizes to \texttt{G2 X3.275 R0.083}.

\paragraph{Variable-precision coordinate strings.}
Step~4 is the only normalization step that touches numeric values, and it applies only to command leading zeros; trailing-zero differences on coordinate strings (\texttt{X3.2} vs.\ \texttt{X3.2000}) are \emph{not} resolved at canonicalization. They are made equivalent at the next stage: quantization (below) maps both to the same integer bucket at the parameter's precision (in this case, $\text{round}(3.2 / 0.001) = \text{round}(3.2000 / 0.001) = 3200$, emitted as \texttt{NUM\_X\_3200}). The tokenizer is therefore firmware-agnostic: TinyG (the controller on our Bantam Tools Desktop CNC), grbl, and other firmwares that interpret trailing zeros differently in their parsers all receive identical tokens from our preprocessing, because the distinction is absorbed by bucket-level quantization before any model ever sees a coordinate.

\subsubsection{Hybrid Tokenization}

We use a \emph{hybrid} strategy. Literal mode creates one token per coordinate value (vocabulary explosion). Split mode over-fragments commands (\texttt{G1} $\to$ \texttt{G}, \texttt{1}). Hybrid mode preserves command semantics while separating addresses from values:

\begin{lstlisting}
G2 X3.275 Y0.375 R0.083
-> [BOS, G2, X, NUM_X_3275, Y,
    NUM_Y_375, R, NUM_R_830, EOS]
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Quantization}

We quantize numeric values at domain-appropriate precision, mapping a float value $v$ for parameter $a$ to an integer bucket:
\begin{equation}
    \text{bucket}(v, a) = \text{round}\!\left(\frac{v}{\text{precision}(a)}\right)
\end{equation}

\noindent For example, \texttt{X3.275} $\to$ $\text{round}(3.275/0.001) = 3275$ $\to$ \texttt{NUM\_X\_3275}; \texttt{R0.083} $\to$ $\text{round}(0.083/0.0001) = 830$ $\to$ \texttt{NUM\_R\_830}.

\subsection{Hierarchical token decomposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

% Decoder architecture overview
\subsubsection{From Flat to Structured Prediction}

A flat vocabulary selects from $|\mathcal{V}|$ classes with no structural bias---the model must independently learn that \texttt{NUM\_X\_3275} and \texttt{NUM\_X\_3276} are numerically adjacent. Our hierarchical approach decomposes prediction into a cascade:
\begin{equation}
    P(\text{token}) = P(\tau) \cdot P(\iota \mid \tau) \cdot P(\mathbf{v} \mid \iota, \tau)
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the token type, $\iota$ is the identity (command or parameter address), and $\mathbf{v}$ is the numeric value. This factorization follows the class-based language model tradition~\cite{brown_classlm,morin_hsoftmax}: tokens are grouped into classes (types), and prediction proceeds hierarchically through class then member. Our contribution is not the factorization itself but its instantiation for G-code: the classes are grammar-derived (not learned), the third level decomposes into digits rather than selecting from a fixed set, and the entire cascade integrates with the DFA-based grammar constraints from Section~\ref{sec:formal_grammar}. Figure~\ref{fig:decomposition_detail} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}) illustrates this cascade with a concrete example.

\subsubsection{Level 1: Token Type (4 classes)}

A four-way classifier determines the token's role---the ``part of speech'' of G-code. This prediction is nearly deterministic given the grammar: after \textsc{Parameter}, the type \emph{must} be \textsc{Numeric}.

\subsubsection{Level 2: Identity Prediction}

Conditioned on token type:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textsc{Command} $\to$ \{G0, G1, G2, G3\} (4 classes)
    \item \textsc{Parameter} $\to$ \{X, Y, Z, F, R, ...\} (5+ classes)
    \item \textsc{Special} $\to$ \{PAD, BOS, EOS, UNK, MASK\} (5 classes)
    \item \textsc{Numeric} $\to$ proceed to Level~3
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Level 3: Digit-by-Digit Numeric Prediction}

Instead of predicting a numeric token as one of hundreds of vocabulary entries, we decompose each value into structured components:

\begin{equation}
    v = s \cdot \left( \sum_{i=0}^{1} d_i \cdot 10^{1-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{3} d_{2+j} \cdot 10^{-(j+1)} \right)
\end{equation}

where $s \in \{+1, -1, 0\}$ is the sign and $d_0, \ldots, d_5 \in \{0, \ldots, 9\}$ are the six digits in the format $\pm d_0 d_1 . d_2 d_3 d_4 d_5$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Digit-by-Digit Decomposition Examples}
\label{tab:digit_examples}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrllc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Token} & \textbf{Val.} & \textbf{Sign} & \textbf{Digits} & \textbf{Format} \\
\midrule
\texttt{NUM\_X\_3291}  & 3.291  & $+$ & $[0,3,2,9,1,0]$ & $+03.2910$ \\

This decomposition provides five advantages: (1)~\textbf{zero OOV}---any value is representable; (2)~\textbf{numeric proximity}---3.275 and 3.276 differ in one digit; (3)~\textbf{compositionality}---each digit is a 10-class problem; (4)~\textbf{precision control}---append digits without vocabulary growth; (5)~\textbf{generalization}---works for any geometry without retraining.

\textbf{Why digits, not regression?} A regression head (like our auxiliary $\hat{v}$) produces a point estimate, losing the ability to express structured uncertainty---e.g., ``confident the integer part is 3, uncertain whether the fourth decimal is 2 or 3.'' Digit heads produce a joint distribution over the value space and integrate with grammar-based logit masking, which has no analogue for continuous outputs. xVal~\cite{xval} takes the regression approach, preserving magnitude but losing both digit-level structure and constraint compatibility.

\textbf{Limitation: digit independence.} The six heads predict independently. To characterize the cost of this assumption, we compute the mutual information (MI) between adjacent digit positions across 13{,}595 numeric values in our dataset:

\begin{center}
\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Pair} & $d_0$-$d_1$ & $d_1$-$d_2$ & $d_2$-$d_3$ & $d_3$-$d_4$ \\
\midrule
MI (bits)  & 0.01 & 0.85 & 0.45 & 0.09 \\
NMI        & 0.86 & 0.87 & 0.17 & 0.03 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The integer-decimal boundary ($d_1$-$d_2$) exhibits strong dependency (0.85 bits), as expected: knowing the integer part constrains the decimal range. Later decimal positions ($d_3$-$d_5$) are near-independent (MI $< 0.2$ bits, NMI $< 0.06$). An autoregressive chain predicting $d_0 \to d_1 \to d_2$ then independent $d_3$-$d_5$ could capture the high-MI boundary while keeping most predictions parallel.

\subsubsection{Conditioning Signals}

Digit prediction is conditioned on the \textbf{parameter type} (X, Y, Z, F, R---determining physical quantity and range) and \textbf{operation type} (face, pocket, adaptive---influencing typical value distributions). These embeddings are concatenated with the hidden state and projected before digit prediction.

\subsubsection{Training Objective}

The hierarchical decomposition yields a multi-head loss:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_\tau + \mathcal{L}_\iota + \mathcal{L}_s + \sum_{i=0}^{5} \mathcal{L}_{d_i} + \lambda_{\text{aux}} \mathcal{L}_{\hat{v}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{grammar}}
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_\tau$, $\mathcal{L}_\iota$, $\mathcal{L}_s$, $\mathcal{L}_{d_i}$ are cross-entropy losses on type (4), identity, sign (3), and digit (10) predictions; $\mathcal{L}_{\hat{v}}$ is Huber loss on an auxiliary scalar prediction ($\lambda_{\text{aux}} = 0.1$); and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{grammar}}$ sums soft constraint penalties (Section~\ref{sec:constrained_decoding}). Losses are masked to apply only at positions of the corresponding type.

\subsubsection{Token Reassembly}

Given predictions from all levels, a G-code token is reassembled deterministically:

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\Function{Reassemble}{$\tau$, $\iota$, $s$, $\mathbf{d}$}
    \If{$\tau \in \{\text{Spcl, Cmd, Par}\}$}
        \State \Return lookup$[\tau, \iota]$  \Comment{e.g.\ \texttt{G2}, \texttt{X}, \texttt{EOS}}
    \ElsIf{$\tau = \text{Num}$}
        \State $v \gets \sum_i d_i \cdot 10^{1-i}$  \Comment{compose digits}
        \State \textbf{if} $s = -$ \textbf{then} $v \gets -v$
        \State $a \gets \text{last\_param\_address}$
        \State \Return \texttt{NUM\_\{$a$\}\_\{round($v$/prec($a$))\}}
    \EndIf
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}

\smallskip\noindent This closes the loop: hierarchical predictions $\to$ digits $\to$ G-code token $\to$ valid line.

\paragraph{Inference-time overhead.}
The hierarchical head cascade replaces a single $d_{\text{model}} \to 712$ softmax with nine smaller projections ($d_{\text{model}} \to \{4, 14, 3\}$ plus six $d_{\text{model}} \to 10$ digit heads). On a CPU micro-benchmark ($d_{\text{model}} = 384$, single-sample forward pass, 1000-iteration mean), the flat head costs $\approx\!38\,\mu$s/token and the hierarchical cascade costs $\approx\!73\,\mu$s/token---a $+35\,\mu$s overhead per token, roughly $+93\%$ over the flat head. In absolute terms this is still well below real-time CNC monitoring latency budgets (at 50\,Hz sensor sampling, one window-to-token inference per 20\,ms leaves $\sim 270\times$ headroom), and the overhead is dominated by kernel launch cost on small tensors; on GPU or with batched inference the relative cost shrinks because the heads can run in parallel. We therefore treat the cascade overhead as negligible for monitoring applications and note it only for completeness.

\subsection{Grammar-constrained decoding}
\label{sec:constrained_decoding}

We enforce the grammar via hard constraints (inference-time logit masking) and soft constraints (training-time penalties).

\subsubsection{Hard Constraints via Logit Masking}

The logit mask implements the DFA transition function from Section~\ref{sec:formal_grammar}. Define $M: Q \times \Sigma \to \{0, -\infty\}$ as:
\begin{equation}
    M(q, t) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \delta(q, t) \text{ is defined} \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
At each decoding step, the masked logits are $\tilde{\ell}^{(t)} = \ell^{(t)} + M(q_t, \cdot)$ where $q_t$ is the current DFA state. By construction, the mask is \textbf{sound}: every generated sequence is in $L(\mathcal{A}) = L(\mathcal{G})$, since only transitions defined in $\delta$ have non-zero probability. It is also \textbf{complete}: every string in $L(\mathcal{G})$ can be generated, since the mask never blocks a valid transition.

Table~\ref{tab:type_transitions} shows the mask in tabular form.

Beyond type transitions, command-specific rules further constrain parameter selection:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{G0 $\to$ no F.} Rapid traversals have no programmed feed rate; the \texttt{F} logit is masked to $-\infty$.
    \item \textbf{G2/G3 $\to$ R required.} Arc interpolation demands geometry specification; the \texttt{R} logit receives a $+10$ boost.
    \item \textbf{No repeated addresses.} Parameters already emitted in the current block are masked to $-\infty$.
\end{itemize}

Together, these masks guarantee that every generated sequence is a syntactically valid G-code line.

\subsubsection{Soft Constraints as Training Penalties}

During training, soft penalty terms encourage compliance with domain-specific rules beyond hard syntactic constraints. These define a weighted regular language---each rule $k$ penalizes violating sequences proportionally:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{grammar}} = \sum_{k} \lambda_k \cdot \mathcal{L}_k
\end{equation}

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Soft Grammar Constraint Penalties}
\label{tab:soft_constraints}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcp{3.8cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Constraint} & $\lambda$ & \textbf{Rule} \\
\midrule

Modal group        & 0.6 & One motion cmd per block \\
Rapid feed         & 0.5 & G0 $\to$ no F parameter \\
Arc alternation    & 0.4 & G2 $\leftrightarrow$ G3 in spirals \\
Cmd-param assoc.   & 0.4 & Valid params per command \\

Z-retract pattern  & 0.2 & G0\,Z $\to$ XY reposition \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\normalsize
\end{table}

Weights reflect physical severity. An arc without radius ($\lambda=1.0$) produces an unexecutable instruction---the controller will fault. A rapid with feed rate ($\lambda=0.5$) is accepted by some controllers but semantically meaningless. Z-retract ordering ($\lambda=0.2$) is a stylistic convention, not a hard rule. These values derive from machining domain knowledge and have not been tuned by grid search. Two observations make this acceptable in practice. First, hard masks enforce correctness at inference regardless, so the soft weights shape only the training-time gradient and cannot introduce grammar violations at generation. Second, the on/off ablation ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{grammar}} = 0$ vs.\ the full weighted sum, Section~\ref{sec:baseline_performance}) yields a $+1.4$-pp mean sequence-accuracy gain that is within fold-level noise, suggesting that the published model's accuracy is not sensitive to the specific weight values. We interpret this as the soft constraints pulling the model toward domain-consistent parameter patterns during training but providing most of their operational value through the hard mask at inference time. A proper sensitivity sweep (e.g., randomized weight perturbations with paired-fold evaluation) is future work.

\subsubsection{When Constraints Can Hurt}

Hard constraints guarantee syntax but can propagate commitment errors: if the model is uncertain between \texttt{G1} and \texttt{G2}, choosing \texttt{G2} triggers the arc-radius requirement, forcing an \texttt{R} prediction the model may not be prepared to make. Beam search with deferred commitment could mitigate this. Additionally, our grammar covers only G0--G3; valid constructs outside this subset would be incorrectly masked.

\section{Empirical Validation}
\label{sec:empirical}

We validate the framework's claims on 120 G-code files (62{,}470 lines total, 2{,}248 unique) from Autodesk Fusion~360 CAM, covering face milling, pocket milling, and adaptive clearing on a Bantam Tools Desktop CNC mill. The empirical work serves two distinct purposes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item Section~\ref{sec:baseline_performance} establishes how the published hierarchical model compares to standard baselines (tokenizer comparison, grammar coverage, vocabulary growth, and downstream decoder performance).
    \item Sections~\ref{sec:design_space_exp}--\ref{sec:kl_measurement} \emph{test the definitions and the trichotomy} of Section~\ref{sec:design_space}: Experiments~A--D probe the three properties of Definitions~\ref{def:bounded}--\ref{def:grammar_valid} across seven tokenizer families and confirm Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} cell-by-cell; Experiments~C and~E test what happens when a trained decoder is asked to free-generate under each tokenizer; and the KL-divergence measurement (Section~\ref{sec:kl_measurement}) tests whether grammar-constrained decoding under our framework distorts the model distribution in the regime ASAp~\cite{grammar_aligned} was designed for.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Canonical-line overlap between train and test splits.}
Because CAM-generated G-code is highly repetitive (62{,}470 raw lines collapse to 2{,}248 unique canonical forms---a 28:1 ratio; Section~\ref{sec:program_structure}), a large share of canonical lines that appear in a held-out test split also appear in the corresponding training split, even though the splits respect \emph{program} boundaries (no run from a held-out program leaks into training; Appendix~\ref{sec:repro_data}). We measured this directly across the 5 folds of the sensor-aligned experiment: mean canonical-line overlap rate between test and train is $93.6\%$ (range $79$--$100\%$ across folds). Experiments~C and~E should therefore be read as ``given a sensor window, reconstruct a canonical line the model has likely seen during training'' rather than ``given a sensor window, produce a novel canonical line.'' This is a realistic scenario for line-level process verification (the problem is distinguishing which of a small set of known canonical primitives is currently executing) but it is a weaker generalization test than the cross-operation OOV evaluation in Experiment~D (Section~\ref{sec:design_space_exp}), which is what we rely on for claims about novel geometry. Experiment~D uses disjoint \emph{operation types} for train and test and therefore measures how each tokenizer handles genuinely unseen coordinate values.

\subsection{Testing the three properties (Experiments A, B, D)}
\label{sec:design_space_exp}

This subsection tests Definitions~\ref{def:bounded}--\ref{def:grammar_valid} and Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} directly by populating every cell of Table~\ref{tab:design_space} with a real measurement on the 120-file corpus. Each experiment maps one-to-one onto a definition:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item Experiment~A tests Definition~\ref{def:bounded} (\textsc{Bounded}) by measuring active vocabulary growth on every tokenizer family.
    \item Experiment~B tests Definition~\ref{def:lossless} (\textsc{Lossless}) by measuring coordinate roundtrip error.
    \item Experiment~D tests the operational consequence of failing \textsc{Bounded} on novel geometries by measuring held-out OOV across cross-geometry splits.
\end{itemize}
The test of property~(i) of Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} (\textsc{Grammar}) requires a trained model and is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:exp_c}.

\paragraph{Experiment A: Vocabulary growth.}
For each tokenizer we walk the 120 \texttt{*\_aligned.csv} files in lexical order and record the cumulative number of distinct token ids actually emitted on encode (active vocabulary). Pretrained tokenizers (GPT-4, GPT-2) have fixed capacity but their active vocabulary grows as new substrings are observed; data-trained tokenizers (Domain BPE, WordPiece, Flat per-value) build vocabulary as they encounter the corpus. Table~\ref{tab:exp_a_growth} reports the active vocabulary at four file counts.

\input{design_space_results/table_a.tex}

The hierarchical tokenizer and character-level tokenizer plateau immediately at 25 and 26 active tokens respectively. The 25 for hierarchical reflects the small structural alphabet (Table~\ref{tab:vocab_complete}: 7--9 tokens active in this dataset) plus the 11-element digit/sign sub-vocabulary that the model emits. Table~\ref{tab:tokenizer_comparison} reports only the 19 structural slots: numeric values are predicted by the digit heads rather than vocabulary entries (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}). The two counts are consistent under different definitions of ``vocabulary.'' The BPE and WordPiece variants are nominally bounded but use 700--993 active tokens---roughly $40\times$ the structural vocabulary needed by Definition~\ref{def:bounded}. The flat per-value tokenizer grows monotonically from 520 to 4{,}435 tokens across 120 files with no sign of saturation; the curve approximately follows Heaps' law~\cite{heaps1978} with $\beta \approx 0.42$ across 16 sampled checkpoints (Table~\ref{tab:vocab_growth}). \textsc{Bounded} therefore holds for every row except flat per-value, exactly as Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy} predicts.

\paragraph{Experiment B: Coordinate reconstruction (in-distribution sanity).}
We extract every unique \emph{(address, value)} pair from the canonicalized corpus (4{,}418 pairs across X/Y/Z/R/F) and round-trip each value through every tokenizer's encode--decode. Table~\ref{tab:exp_b_recon} reports the mean absolute reconstruction error in the address's native unit.

\input{design_space_results/table_b.tex}

This experiment serves as a sanity check rather than a differentiator: encode--decode is symbolically lossless for every tokenizer that retains the input string verbatim, so BPE variants, character-level, and flat per-value all achieve zero error on values that already appear in the training corpus. Our hierarchical tokenizer attains a mean error of $\approx\!0.25\,\mu$m on X/Y/Z---exactly half the quantization step $p = 1\,\mu$m, which is the theoretical lower bound for any precision-quantized representation and is well below the positional accuracy of the Bantam Tools Desktop CNC mill ($\approx\!10\,\mu$m). WordPiece exhibits a small but non-zero reconstruction error ($\approx\!2.6\,\mu$m mean) attributable to whitespace normalization in the HuggingFace decoder. The differentiator between tokenizers is not in-distribution roundtrip but cross-distribution generalization, which Experiment~D addresses directly.

\paragraph{Experiment D: Cross-geometry OOV (the principal experiment).}
We split the corpus by operation type---face milling, pocket milling, and adaptive clearing---and for each fold ``train'' the tokenizer on two operations and evaluate on the third. For tokenizers without an explicit training step, we instead populate the active vocabulary by encoding the training fold and then count, on the held-out fold, how many emitted token ids fall outside the seen vocabulary. Table~\ref{tab:exp_d_oov} reports the held-out OOV rate.

\input{design_space_results/table_d.tex}

The ranking is unambiguous. The hierarchical and character-level tokenizers achieve essentially zero OOV across all folds---both decompose unseen values into a finite alphabet that is closed under composition. Domain-trained BPE and WordPiece remain in single-digit-percent territory but are not OOV-free; GPT-4's BPE reaches 4.81\% on held-out adaptive operations (which contain the most geometrically diverse coordinates), and the flat per-value tokenizer collapses entirely (18--65\% OOV across folds). This is the empirical signature of Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy}: any tokenizer that satisfies bounded vocabulary by enumerating values (flat) or by data-driven subword merges (BPE/WordPiece) loses lossless representation on novel geometries, while only digit-decomposed and character-level tokenizers preserve it. Of those two, only the hierarchical decomposition also satisfies grammar validity at the value level (Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid}, property~(ii)), since character-level FSAs over digit strings explode combinatorially before grammar productions can attach.

Together, Experiments~A, B, and~D populate every cell of the design-space table and confirm that the (\textsc{Bounded}, \textsc{Lossless}, \textsc{Grammar}) corner is occupied by exactly one row.

\subsection{Free-generation grammar validity (Experiment C)}
\label{sec:exp_c}

This subsection tests property~(i) of Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid}: whether a trained decoder under each tokenizer family can produce encodings that decode back to grammatical lines. Experiments~A and~D establish what each tokenizer \emph{can} represent in principle; Experiment~C measures what a trained model actually emits when freely generating new lines.

We train one decoder per tokenizer family on each of the five v7 cross-validation folds (approximately 303 train / 110 val / 130 test sensor-aligned samples per fold). Every arm uses an identical \texttt{FlatVocabDecoder} architecture (192-dim, 4 layers, 8 heads), the same pre-cached encoder memory (sensor\_dim $= 256$), and the same vanilla cross-entropy training schedule (Adam, 80 epochs, lr $3{\times}10^{-4}$, dropout 0.3, batch size 32).

\textbf{The hierarchical row uses the same 712-token vocabulary as the published baseline but is retrained with this slim trainer.} This is intentional. A cross-tokenizer comparison is only meaningful if every row is trained identically, so we strip out the curriculum learning, weighted sampling, focal losses, and data augmentation that the published model relies on, \emph{and} we replace the published model's hierarchical three-head cascade with a single flat softmax over the 712-token vocabulary---the same \texttt{FlatVocabDecoder} architecture used by every other row. That means the slim ``hierarchical'' row is really ``flat decoder over the hybrid 712-vocab,'' and the gap to the published $94.7\%$ has two sources: (a) training schedule (curriculum vs.\ vanilla cross-entropy) and (b) architecture (hierarchical three-head cascade vs.\ flat softmax). We do not decompose the gap because it would require retraining the published model's architecture under the slim schedule, which is the opposite of what this comparison is designed to do.

For reference, the mean slim-trainer test token accuracy across folds is (as percentages, mean $\pm$ std): Domain BPE (whitespace) $89.07 \pm 1.74$, Domain BPE (byte-level) $90.59 \pm 2.72$, WordPiece $89.38 \pm 2.43$, character-level $93.79 \pm 1.26$, flat per-value $82.11 \pm 3.01$, GPT-4 compact $91.58 \pm 1.92$, and hierarchical (slim flat) $85.03 \pm 3.57$. Sequence-level exact-match accuracy is tightly clustered at $48$--$53\%$ across all rows and is not discriminative. These numbers confirm that within the slim regime the differences between tokenizers on token accuracy are modest and do not predict the grammar-validity ranking: character-level has the highest slim token accuracy but the sub-case~2a failure of whitespace-pre-tokenized BPE is invisible at the token-accuracy level (BPE is still $89\%$ accurate at the token level while producing $0\%$ grammatically valid output). The grammar-validity behavior reported in Table~\ref{tab:exp_c} is therefore a genuinely structural property of the tokenizer and decoder pair, not a proxy for generic model accuracy.

For each test sample we free-generate one G-code line conditioned on its sensor memory (no teacher forcing, temperature~$=1.0$, no top-$k$), decode the generated id sequence back to text via the tokenizer's native \texttt{decode}, and check whether the decoded line is syntactically valid against the regular grammar of Section~\ref{sec:formal_grammar}. Table~\ref{tab:exp_c} reports the grammar-validity rate aggregated across all five folds.

The result separates cleanly into two groups. \paragraph{Statistical significance.} Within the non-failing group the differences are small. A paired bootstrap over the 5 folds (10{,}000 resamples, seed 42) finds that the hierarchical row is not significantly better than byte-level BPE, character-level, or GPT-4 compact (one-sided $p = 0.153$, $0.235$, $0.118$ respectively); the $+1.33$-pp gap over flat per-value is marginally significant ($p = 0.016$, $95\%$ CI $[+0.08, +2.43]$ pp). The failure mode for the standard BPE row is a property of the decoder configuration rather than of BPE as a class. Inspection of generated samples shows that the model correctly predicts subwords corresponding to values like $1.4919$, but the HuggingFace BPE decoder---using the \texttt{Whitespace} pre-tokenizer's inverse---reassembles those subwords with whitespace at every BPE boundary, producing outputs like \texttt{"G0 X1 . 4919 Y1 . 4848"} instead of \texttt{"G0 X1.4919 Y1.4848"}. A regex-based G-code parser rejects the spaced version even though every subword is correct. This is sub-case~2a from the case analysis in Section~\ref{sec:impossibility}: a deterministic decode-side failure, not an intrinsic limitation of BPE merges.

The byte-level BPE control isolates sub-case~2a from sub-case~2b (merge-table ambiguity). Replacing the \texttt{Whitespace} pre-tokenizer and decoder with \texttt{ByteLevel} makes the encode--decode roundtrip bit-exact on the canonicalized input: a freshly trained byte-level BPE round-trips \texttt{R0.1755} to \texttt{R0.1755} with no whitespace artifact. Under that configuration, grammar validity rises to $97.14 \pm 1.55\%$, statistically indistinguishable from the other non-fragmenting tokenizers. \emph{This is a negative result for sub-case~2b}: the merge-table's context-dependent fragmentation does not cause grammar failure in practice on our corpus. Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy}'s Case~2 argument now reduces to: the out-of-the-box BPE pipelines used in practice (with a \texttt{Whitespace} pre-tokenizer) deterministically fail Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} property~(i), but a non-standard byte-level BPE configuration passes on this corpus---although it still fails Definition~\ref{def:bounded} in practice (100--1000 learned merges that grow with geometry) and Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} property~(ii) at the value level (the constraint mechanism has no consistent token boundary at which to apply a per-value mask). The all-checkmarks corner of Table~\ref{tab:design_space} is therefore still occupied only by digit decomposition; byte-level BPE is better than the whitespace variant but does not satisfy all three properties simultaneously.

\subsection{Toolpath reconstruction error (Experiment E)}
\label{sec:exp_e}

Experiment~C is a test of property~(i) of Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} at the symbolic level: do decoded outputs parse? Experiment~E asks the dual question in physical units: when they don't parse, how much does the resulting toolpath deviate from ground truth? It measures the downstream consequence of grammar failure in the unit machinists actually care about. For each test sample, we parse both the ground-truth and free-generated lines through the same \texttt{GCodeToCoordinateParser}~(Section~\ref{sec:related}), reconstruct the implied 3D toolpath, and compute the bidirectional Hausdorff distance between the two point sets:
\[
H(A, B) = \max\!\Big(\sup_{a \in A}\inf_{b \in B}\|a-b\|,\;\sup_{b \in B}\inf_{a \in A}\|a-b\|\Big).
\]
The parser treats both \texttt{G0} and \texttt{G1} as linear interpolation between the prior and next endpoints.\footnote{Real CNC controllers typically execute \texttt{G0} rapid traversals with independent-axis motion rather than coordinated linear interpolation, which produces a ``dog-leg'' path that deviates from a straight line by up to $\sqrt{2}\cdot d/2$ at the midpoint of a diagonal move of length $d$. Our linear-interpolation approximation is consistent with how most simulators and CAM previews render \texttt{G0} and does not affect the grammar-validity metric (Experiment~C), which is evaluated at the symbolic level before any geometric reconstruction. It may introduce a small systematic bias in the Experiment~E Hausdorff distances, but the bias is common across all tokenizer rows and therefore does not affect the cross-tokenizer ranking.}
Table~\ref{tab:exp_e} reports the per-tokenizer mean, median, and 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance in millimeters, aggregated across all five cross-validation folds.

The Hausdorff result is more nuanced than~C and worth interpreting carefully. The mean and p95 cluster across all tokenizers because the test set contains a small number of geometrically diverse samples on which every slim-trainer model produces poor reconstructions; these dominate the upper percentiles. The discriminative statistic is the \emph{median}, where two groups emerge:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item Tokenizers that pass Experiment~C (byte-level BPE, character-level, flat per-value, GPT-4 compact, hierarchical) achieve median Hausdorff between $0.16$ and $0.47$\,mm. The tightest median is char-level at $0.163$\,mm; the hierarchical slim-trainer row sits at $0.473$\,mm, reflecting its lower absolute token accuracy in the slim regime rather than a tokenizer-side disadvantage.
    \item Tokenizers that fail Experiment~C (Domain BPE with whitespace pre-tokenizer, WordPiece) sit at median $0.62$--$0.64$\,mm, roughly $2$--$4\times$ larger than the non-failing group, because their malformed outputs cannot be parsed into points at all and the parser falls back to the start position.
\end{itemize}
The grammar-failure penalty is therefore not a cosmetic syntactic concern: it propagates directly into hundreds of microns of toolpath error per line, which on a multi-thousand-line program compounds catastrophically. Notably, the byte-level BPE row joins the non-failing group (median $0.317$\,mm), confirming that the toolpath-error gap is explained by decoder-side whitespace reinsertion rather than by BPE's merge table.

The intent of Experiments~C and~E is to isolate the tokenizer's contribution under fixed training conditions, and that comparison is unambiguous across all 5 folds: \emph{any tokenizer that fragments coordinate strings is unrecoverable at generation time, regardless of how well the model itself learns}. The 5-fold standard deviations on the BPE/WordPiece grammar-validity rate ($0.00 \pm 0.00$) confirm this is a deterministic structural failure, not a fold-dependent artifact.

\subsection{Distribution distortion under grammar masking}
\label{sec:kl_measurement}

Park et al.~\cite{grammar_aligned} show that na\"ive grammar-constrained decoding can distort the underlying model distribution and propose ASAp as a corrective. To check whether this concern is operationally relevant for our system, we measure the per-step KL divergence between the model's grammar-masked and unmasked type-head distributions on the held-out test sets across all five cross-validation folds. At each non-pad position $t$, we compute
\[
\text{KL}_t = \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_t + M_t)\,\|\,\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_t)\right),
\]
where $\ell_t \in \mathbb{R}^4$ are the type-head logits and $M_t$ is the DFA-derived type-level mask from Section~\ref{sec:formal_grammar}. We also report the \emph{blocked probability mass}: the share of the unmasked distribution that lies on grammar-disallowed types.

The mean KL divergence of $0.005$ nats ($\approx 0.007$ bits) is negligible: it corresponds to a multiplicative shift factor of $\exp(0.005) \approx 1.005$ on the most-affected token's probability. The unmasked model already places $99.86\%$ of its type-prediction mass on grammar-valid types---a direct consequence of training under the type-head loss with implicit grammar exposure---so the mask removes only the residual $0.14\%$. ASAp-style corrective methods are designed for the regime in which a substantial share of the unmasked model's probability mass falls on grammar-disallowed tokens; our observed blocked-mass fraction is two orders of magnitude smaller than that. This empirically supports our $O(1)$ hard-mask design choice (Section~\ref{sec:constrained_decoding}) and confirms that, for grammars whose transitions are near-deterministic given a sufficiently trained model, the Park~et~al.\ critique is muted in practice.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central insight of the paper is that G-code separates cleanly into a structural component (type, command, parameter identity) that is small and discrete, and a numeric component (coordinate values) that is continuous and unbounded. These demand different mechanisms, and our downstream results confirm the split: structural predictions exceed 96\% accuracy while numeric digit prediction reaches 91.1\%, a five-point gap that validates the decomposition (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}). Existing systems conflate the two: GLLM~\cite{gllm} applies uniform BPE over both commands and coordinates, and Image2Gcode~\cite{image2gcode} treats everything as continuous. The same separation should apply to any numeric-heavy domain-specific language, including robot motion programs, 3D printer instructions, PLC ladder logic, and other CNC dialects.

The paper also extends Technical Language Processing from human-written prose to formally specified machine languages. Prior TLP work~\cite{tlp_cleaning,tlp_keyword,tlp_semiautonomous,tlp_nuclear,tlp_fault,tlp_foundational,tlp_adapting} addresses lexical failure modes in maintenance logs, equipment reports, and fault summaries, where the challenge is jargon, abbreviation, and inconsistent terminology. G-code presents a different class of failure: it is formally specified, machine generated, and numerically dense, and its tokenization failure is structural rather than lexical (Table~\ref{tab:tlp_comparison}). Whether G-code is representative of a broader category that includes RAPID, KRL, PLC structured text, and STEP-NC, or an outlier among formally specified technical languages, requires investigation across multiple languages.

The most consequential finding is the cleanly separated behavior of BPE under two pre-tokenizer configurations. Standard whitespace-pre-tokenized BPE and WordPiece collapse to $0.00 \pm 0.00\%$ free-generation grammar validity across 5 folds, because the decoder reassembles subwords with whitespace at every fragmentation boundary and the resulting string is unparseable. Byte-level BPE, trained on the same corpus with the same vocabulary budget, recovers to $97.14\%$---statistically indistinguishable from the non-fragmenting rows. The merge-table ambiguity that Proposition~\ref{prop:trichotomy}'s Case~2 argued would cause grammar failure (sub-case~2b) does not materialize in practice on our corpus: the actual failure lives entirely in the decoder configuration (sub-case~2a). This is an honest narrowing of what the BPE critique supports. The all-checkmarks corner of Table~\ref{tab:design_space} is still occupied only by digit decomposition, but via a different mechanism: byte-level BPE satisfies Definition~\ref{def:grammar_valid} property~(i) yet still fails Definition~\ref{def:bounded} (its active vocabulary grows with geometry) and the value-level form of property~(ii) (no consistent token boundary at which to apply a per-value constraint mask).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several boundaries to our claims should be noted. \textbf{Grammar scope:} the grammar covers only the four core motion commands (G0--G3) with five parameter addresses (\texttt{X}, \texttt{Y}, \texttt{Z}, \texttt{F}, \texttt{R}); a production system would need \texttt{I}/\texttt{J}/\texttt{K} arc-center format (the default on many controllers, and required for arcs exceeding $180^\circ$), canned cycles (\texttt{G81}--\texttt{G89}), tool compensation (\texttt{G41}/\texttt{G42}), coordinate systems beyond \texttt{G54}, tool management, and controller-specific dialects (Fanuc, Siemens, Mazak, Haas). \textbf{Machine envelope and precision:} our dataset is from a desktop-class 3-axis mill with a $\sim$140$\times$102$\times$60\,mm work envelope, and the per-parameter precision in Table~\ref{tab:precision} is chosen accordingly ($0.001$\,mm on \texttt{X}/\texttt{Y}/\texttt{Z}; $0.0001$\,mm on \texttt{R}). Production CNC work routinely involves larger ranges ($\pm 2000$\,mm on gantry mills), finer nominal precisions ($0.1\,\mu$m in aerospace finishing), or coarser ones ($0.1$\,mm in woodworking); the digit-head configuration scales by adjusting the integer- and decimal-digit counts per address in Table~\ref{tab:precision} without structural changes to the framework, but we have not evaluated this empirically. \textbf{Firmware scope:} our canonicalizer is firmware-agnostic---Section~\ref{sec:tokenization} shows that variable-precision strings like \texttt{X3.2} and \texttt{X3.2000} collapse to the same token bucket under both TinyG (the controller on our Bantam mill) and grbl---but we have not tested against Fanuc, Siemens, Haas, or Mazak dialects, which use different comment syntaxes, subroutine conventions, and modal group definitions. \textbf{Digit independence:} the assumption is justified for later decimal positions (mutual information $< 0.2$ bits) but breaks down at the integer-decimal boundary ($d_{1}$--$d_{2}$: 0.85 bits); a partial autoregressive chain could address this. \textbf{Dataset scope:} the grammar coverage evaluation uses the same dataset from which the grammar was designed, so 99.9\% coverage is descriptive of this corpus, not predictive of unseen CAM output; held-out evaluation across CAM systems (Mastercam, SolidCAM) and controllers is needed to assess generalization. Canonical-line overlap between train and test splits averages $93.6\%$ across folds (Section~\ref{sec:empirical}), so Experiments~C and~E should be read as verification rather than novel-line generation; the held-out OOV experiment (Experiment~D) uses disjoint operation types and provides the principal generalization test. \textbf{Soft-constraint weights:} the values in Table~\ref{tab:soft_constraints} were set from domain knowledge without a sensitivity analysis, and the grammar-constraint ablation shows a modest, non-significant effect ($+1.4$ pp) that suggests the constraints' value lies more in guaranteeing validity than in improving accuracy. \textbf{Slim-trainer caveat for Experiments C and E:} the cross-tokenizer experiments retrain the hierarchical row with a vanilla cross-entropy slim trainer rather than the published curriculum-learning pipeline, so its test token accuracy sits below the published 94.7\%. This is the cost of an apples-to-apples comparison and does not affect the cross-tokenizer ranking, which is determined by structural properties of the tokenizer rather than by the training schedule.

\paragraph{Future work.}
We see five directions: (1)~held-out evaluation on files from different CAM systems (Mastercam, SolidCAM) and controllers (Fanuc, Siemens); (2)~extending the grammar to the full RS-274D standard, including canned cycles and multi-line program structure; (3)~autoregressive digit chains and regression-loss alternatives~\cite{regress_dont_guess} for numeric prediction; (4)~integration with generation systems such as GLLM~\cite{gllm} to evaluate whether grammar-aware tokenization improves G-code synthesis; and (5)~application to related manufacturing languages (STEP-NC, 3D printer G-code, Fanuc and Siemens dialects) to test the generality of the approach.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Standard tokenization fails on G-code because its vocabulary is 97\% numeric and scales with geometry. The flat vocabulary grows from 527 to 4{,}444 tokens across 120 files, while the structural vocabulary remains fixed at 19.

We addressed this with three contributions. First, a formal regular grammar (5-state DFA, soundness and completeness proven), a hierarchical class-based decomposition~\cite{brown_classlm}, and digit-by-digit numeric prediction that eliminates vocabulary explosion by construction. Second, a structural characterization of the G-code tokenizer design space (Section~\ref{sec:design_space}): among the three dominant tokenizer classes (atomic, subword, digit-decomposed), only digit decomposition simultaneously satisfies bounded vocabulary, lossless coordinate representation, and value-level grammar validity. The framework is a principled choice among the standard families rather than one design among many. Third, an empirical battery that populates every cell of the resulting design-space table, including a byte-level BPE control that isolates decoder-configuration artifacts from the structural property.

Vocabulary growth and held-out OOV (Experiments~A and~D) confirm that flat per-value tokenizers grow without bound and that BPE and WordPiece variants OOV at 0.25--4.81\% on novel geometries, while only digit-decomposed and character-level tokenizers remain below $0.001\%$. Free-generation grammar validity (Experiment~C) collapses to $0.00 \pm 0.00\%$ for the standard-pipeline BPE and WordPiece rows across 5 cross-validation folds and more than 660 test samples, because the HuggingFace whitespace-pre-tokenized decoder reinserts spaces at every fragmentation boundary. A byte-level BPE control that avoids this decode-time artifact recovers to $97.14\%$---within the band of the other non-fragmenting tokenizers ($96.98$--$98.31\%$), confirming that the standard-BPE failure is a fixable decoder configuration issue rather than an intrinsic limitation of subword tokenization on this corpus. The KL divergence between grammar-masked and unmasked output distributions is $0.005 \pm 0.003$ nats, with only $0.14\%$ of the unmasked probability mass on grammar-disallowed types; this is two orders of magnitude below the regime where ASAp-style corrections become necessary, neutralizing the chief critique of $O(1)$ hard-mask designs.

The published hierarchical decoder achieves 94.7\% token accuracy and 86.5\% sequence exact-match in 5-fold cross-validation, a 19.8-point improvement over a flat 712-class baseline on the same architecture. Structural predictions exceed 96\% while numeric digit prediction (91.1\%) remains the bottleneck, validating that the separation principle works in practice. The broader approach is to separate the easy structural problem from the hard numeric one, handle each with the appropriate mechanism, and treat tokenizer design as a structural problem rather than an engineering problem. We expect this approach to apply to other numeric-heavy domain-specific languages where models must produce syntactically valid, numerically precise output.

\section{Full DFA construction}
\label{sec:dfa_construction}

This appendix gives the deterministic finite automaton that recognizes the regular grammar of Section~\ref{sec:formal_grammar}. The automaton $\mathcal{A} = (Q, \Sigma, \delta, q_0, F)$ has five states $Q = \{q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3, q_4\}$, start state $q_0$, and accepting state $F = \{q_4\}$. Table~\ref{tab:dfa_states} gives the state semantics.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{DFA States and Semantics}
\label{tab:dfa_states}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}cll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{State} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Expects next} \\
\midrule
$q_0$ & Start        & \texttt{BOS} \\
$q_1$ & After BOS    & Command, Address, or \texttt{EOS} \\
$q_2$ & After Cmd    & Address or \texttt{EOS} \\
$q_3$ & After Addr   & \texttt{NUM} only \\
$q_4$ & Accept       & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The transition function $\delta$ has seven non-trivial entries:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
$\delta(q_0, \texttt{BOS}) = q_1$ & (begin line) \\
$\delta(q_1, c) = q_2$, $c \in \{\texttt{G0}\ldots\texttt{G3}\}$ & (command) \\
$\delta(q_1, a) = q_3$, $a \in \mathit{Addr}$ & (modal: no cmd) \\
$\delta(q_2, a) = q_3$, $a \in \mathit{Addr}$ & (parameter) \\
$\delta(q_3, \texttt{NUM}) = q_2$ & (value $\to$ next param) \\
$\delta(q_1, \texttt{EOS}) = q_4$ & (empty block) \\
$\delta(q_2, \texttt{EOS}) = q_4$ & (end after cmd/value) \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Soundness.} Every string accepted by $\mathcal{A}$ lies in $L(\mathcal{G})$: state $q_3$ forces every address to be followed by exactly one \texttt{NUM}; commands (via $q_1 \to q_2$) are optional but must precede parameters when present; and no other orderings are reachable.

\paragraph{Completeness.} Every derivation in $\mathcal{G}$ maps to a unique accepting path in $\mathcal{A}$.

\paragraph{Cross-line modal state.} Per-line parsing is context-free of modal state. Cross-line behavior--tracking which motion command (G0--G3) is active across blocks--is modeled as a finite-state transducer $\mathcal{T}$ with five states (one per command plus an initial state). The input is a sequence of parsed blocks; the output annotates each implicit-command block with its resolved command. The composition $\mathcal{T} \circ \mathcal{A}$ remains a finite-state machine, so the full-program language is regular. Our implementation passes the modal state as an input to the per-line decoder rather than composing the automata explicitly.

\paragraph{State count under extended modal groups.}
The 5-state DFA here covers only the motion modal group. RS-274D defines at least eight modal groups commonly set once in the preamble and held across the program: motion (G0--G3, plus the optional G38 probes and G80 cancel), plane selection (G17--G19), distance mode (G90/G91), units (G20/G21), work coordinate system (G54--G59.3), feed-rate mode (G93--G95), cutter compensation (G40--G42), and tool length offset (G43/G49). A full-coverage finite-state controller for implicit-modal persistence is therefore the Cartesian product of the per-group automata. Using the group cardinalities above, the worst-case product state count is $|G_{\text{motion}}| \cdot |G_{\text{plane}}| \cdot |G_{\text{distance}}| \cdot |G_{\text{units}}| \cdot |G_{\text{wcs}}| \cdot |G_{\text{feed}}| \cdot |G_{\text{cutter}}| \cdot |G_{\text{tlo}}| \approx 6 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 6 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 7{,}776$ states. This remains finite and the per-step $O(1)$ mask lookup is still theoretically valid, but the flat mask table becomes impractical to build by hand; a production implementation would compile the transducer from the per-group automata (standard product construction) and cache transitions lazily. In the current work this does not arise because the preamble fixes all non-motion modal groups before the first toolpath line, leaving only the motion group to track dynamically.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

We document the dataset, training pipeline, and software environment required to reproduce every result in the paper. Code, vocabulary files, trained checkpoints, and split definitions are released alongside the paper at \url{https://github.com/stephenseacuello/gcode\_fingerprinting}.

\subsection{Dataset and splits}
\label{sec:repro_data}

The corpus comprises 120 sensor-aligned CSV files collected on a Bantam Tools Desktop CNC mill running G-code generated by Autodesk Fusion~360 CAM. The 120 files span nine distinct G-code programs covering three operation types (face milling, pocket milling, adaptive clearing) at three feed-rate settings each, with multiple repetitions per program. The unprocessed corpus contains 62{,}470 toolpath lines of which 2{,}248 are unique after canonicalization. Each CSV row contains 110 sensor features---8 electrical channels for the spindle and three axis motors, 102 inertial channels from six MPU-9250 modules at approximately 50~Hz---plus a \texttt{gcode\_string} column giving the G-code line active during the row's sensor window.

All cross-validation experiments use the same five-fold partition (the ``v7'' fold structure in the released code repository). Each fold contains train, validation, and test splits with approximately 303, 110, and 130 sensor-aligned samples respectively, and the same fold definitions are used for the published hierarchical model (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}), the cross-tokenizer experiments (Tables~\ref{tab:exp_c} and~\ref{tab:exp_e}), and the KL distortion measurement (Table~\ref{tab:kl_distortion}). Files are split by source program so that no run from a held-out program leaks into the training set, which prevents trivial within-program memorization from inflating accuracy.

\subsection{Tokenizer and decoder training}
\label{sec:repro_training}

Domain BPE and WordPiece are trained from scratch on each fold's training split using HuggingFace \texttt{tokenizers} version 0.22 with vocabulary cap 1000 and minimum frequency 2. The special tokens \texttt{[PAD]}, \texttt{[BOS]}, \texttt{[EOS]}, \texttt{[UNK]} are forced to ids 0--3 by ordering them first in the trainer's special-tokens list, and the assignment is verified after training. Character-level uses a fixed 32-character alphabet covering canonicalized G-code text; flat-per-value builds vocabulary lazily from whitespace-separated words; GPT-4 BPE (compact) uses tiktoken's \texttt{cl100k\_base} and remaps the active subset of token ids to a corpus-local id space; the hierarchical row uses the published 712-token vocabulary at \texttt{data/gcode\_vocab\_712.json}, unchanged from the existing model.

Every cross-tokenizer arm in Sections~\ref{sec:exp_c} and~\ref{sec:exp_e} uses an identical \texttt{FlatVocabDecoder}: 192-dim embeddings, four transformer-decoder layers, eight attention heads, $4\times$ FFN expansion, dropout 0.3, sinusoidal positional encoding, and sensor conditioning via cross-attention to a pre-cached encoder memory of dimension 256. Training uses Adam ($\text{lr} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$, no weight decay), batch size 32, 80 epochs, token-level cross-entropy with PAD ignored, and gradient clipping at norm 1.0. We deliberately omit curriculum learning, scheduled sampling, weighted sampling, focal losses, and data augmentation in this slim trainer; these are present in the published curriculum-learning trainer and would confound a cross-tokenizer comparison. Best checkpoint is selected by validation loss. The sensor encoder itself is the published MM-DTAE-LSTM model and is not retrained for the cross-tokenizer experiments; we instead load the cached encoder memory tensors and operation-prediction tensors released alongside the v7 fold structure.

\subsection{Hardware, software, and where each table comes from}
\label{sec:repro_compute}

All cross-tokenizer training runs were performed on a single NVIDIA GPU running CUDA 12.1 and PyTorch 2.5.1. The full 5-fold by 6-tokenizer C/E battery (30 trainings plus 5 evaluation passes) runs end-to-end in approximately 12 minutes of wall time, and the KL distortion measurement is a single forward pass per fold and completes in under one minute total. The published hierarchical model was trained separately with the curriculum-learning pipeline at a few hours per fold (script \texttt{train\_sensor\_multihead.py} in the released code).

All slim-trainer experiments use random seed 42 for PyTorch and NumPy, sample at temperature 1.0 with no top-$k$, and report argmax-decoded outputs after EOS trimming. The BPE and WordPiece grammar-validity rate of $0.00 \pm 0.00\%$ is robust to seed choice because the failure mode is in the decode rule rather than the sampling step. The software stack is Python 3.10, PyTorch 2.5.1, NumPy 1.26, pandas 2.x, HuggingFace tokenizers 0.22, and tiktoken 0.12; the pipeline depends only on the standard PyTorch ecosystem and uses no closed-source components.

For traceability, Tables~\ref{tab:tokenizer_comparison}, \ref{tab:vocab_growth}, and~\ref{tab:downstream} are from the published model release. Table~\ref{tab:exp_a_growth} is generated by \texttt{run\_design\_space.py}, Tables~\ref{tab:exp_c} and~\ref{tab:exp_e} are generated by \texttt{eval\_alt.py} and aggregated across folds by \texttt{aggregate\_5fold.py}, and Table~\ref{tab:kl_distortion} is generated by \texttt{measure\_kl.py}, all under \texttt{scripts/experiments/grammar\_design\_space/} in the released code. The full preprocess--train--evaluate--aggregate pipeline can be reproduced by running \texttt{run\_5fold.sh} from the repository root.
